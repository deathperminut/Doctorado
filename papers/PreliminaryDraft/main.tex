\section{Abstract}

Magnetic domain structures in nanoscale materials play a central role in spintronics, biomedicine, and energy storage technologies. Magnetic nanoparticles and nanodots exhibit complex spin textures—vortices, skyrmions, and stripe domains—whose functional properties depend critically on Hamiltonian parameters such as exchange, anisotropy, Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya interaction, temperature, and external fields. Understanding and controlling these states requires establishing robust mappings between physical parameters and spatial spin configurations.

Three fundamental challenges complicate this effort. First, the nonlinearity and high dimensionality of parameter-to-configuration mappings prevent direct analytical inversion. Second, degeneracy arises when distinct parameter combinations produce visually similar magnetic textures, hindering unique identification. Third, experimental limitations—including noise, finite probe resolution, and tip–sample convolution in microscopy techniques—compromise quantitative interpretation of magnetic domain images.

Deep learning has shown promise in addressing these problems. The inverse problem of inferring physical parameters from magnetic images has been tackled with convolutional neural networks and explainable regression frameworks, while the forward problem of generating domain configurations from parameters has been explored using variational autoencoders and conditional generative adversarial networks. However, critical gaps remain: parameter degeneracy prevents robust inverse estimation, data quality degradation from experimental noise and simulation artifacts limits model reliability, and lack of physics-based interpretability obscures connections between learned representations and fundamental magnetic interactions. Existing generative approaches suffer from poor interpretability, instability under noise, and insufficient physical constraints.

This work proposes a unified deep learning framework that integrates variational methodologies with physics-informed regularization to address degeneracy, bridge the simulation-to-experiment gap, and ensure interpretability. The approach combines image-based parameter estimation with generative modeling of magnetic states, incorporating domain-aware training strategies to maintain validity across synthetic and experimental imaging modalities while employing post-hoc attribution methods to identify spatially relevant features underlying domain configurations.

This research addresses critical national and regional technological needs by advancing Colombia's capabilities in materials characterization and intelligent data processing. The framework supports development of next-generation magnetic memory and energy storage systems, identified as strategic innovation priorities. By integrating physics-informed deep learning with experimental microscopy workflows, this work bridges fundamental materials science and industrial spintronics applications. Furthermore, it aligns with the mission of the Digital Signal and Control Processing Group, extending the group's expertise in digital image processing to nanoscale materials characterization and fostering interdisciplinary collaboration between materials physics, machine learning, and computational imaging.

\section{Motivation}

%1.1. Contexto general: el magnetismo a nanoescala

The study of magnetic systems at the nanoscale has gained strategic importance in
modern materials science \cite{Wang2022}. In particular, magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs) and nanodots
constitute platforms where spin interactions and collective quantum properties give
rise to emergent phenomena of great scientific and technological interest \cite{Rarokar2024}. At these
scales, the balance between exchange interactions, anisotropy, DMI, and external magnetic fields generates a wide variety of magnetic
textures—vortices, skyrmions, helical domains, and labyrinthine configurations \cite{Fert2023}. These
structures determine the magnetic response, thermal stability, and switching dynamics
of the material, which are key aspects for functionality in advanced devices \cite{Song2023}.

%1.2. Relevancia aplicada: por qué las nanopartículas magnéticas importan

Magnetic NPs and nanodots possess a unique versatility that makes them relevant across multiple technological and scientific domains (Figure~\ref{fig:applications}). In the field of spintronics and non-volatile memory technologies, topologically protected magnetic textures—such as skyrmions and magnetic vortices—enable the encoding and manipulation of information as stable, switchable magnetic bits. These nanoscale spin configurations support the development of next-generation memory architectures including MRAM, racetrack memory devices, and reconfigurable magnetic logic gates. The appeal of these technologies lies in their combination of high storage density, ultralow power consumption, radiation hardness, and non-volatility, making them promising candidates for replacing conventional charge-based electronics in applications ranging from edge computing to space-grade systems \cite{Eurek2024}. Beyond information processing, the dynamic magnetic response of these nanostructures under applied fields or current-induced torques allows for fast and deterministic switching, essential for achieving competitive read/write speeds in practical memory devices.

In parallel, magnetic NPs have emerged as transformative agents in biomedical and energy-related applications, where their spin dynamics and domain structure directly govern functional performance. In biomedicine, magnetic NPs are employed in targeted oncological hyperthermia therapies, where alternating magnetic fields induce localized heating to selectively destroy tumor cells, as well as in advanced drug delivery systems that exploit magnetic guidance for site-specific therapeutic action \cite{MLDesign2023}. Their utility extends to contrast enhancement in MRI, where tailored magnetic anisotropy and relaxation properties improve diagnostic sensitivity. In the energy sector, magnetic NPs are investigated for magnetocaloric refrigeration—a sustainable alternative to vapor-compression cooling—thermoelectric energy conversion, and reversible magnetic energy storage systems \cite{He2025}. In all these contexts, the efficiency of the functional response is fundamentally determined by the internal magnetic domain structure, anisotropy landscape, and the mechanisms of energy transfer between spin, lattice, and thermal degrees of freedom \cite{Nehan2024}. This broad spectrum of applications underscores the critical need for robust characterization and predictive modeling frameworks capable of linking Hamiltonian parameters to experimentally observable magnetic configurations.

\section{Problem Statement}
%%%% Experimental and Theoretical Approaches to Magnetic Nanostructures
 
The study of magnetic nanostructures integrates two complementary approaches: the \textbf{experimental} and the \textbf{theoretical-simulation} pathways \cite{Streubel2022}. The experimental route focuses on the physical synthesis, visualization, and measurement of magnetic systems \cite{Zhang2024MFM}, whereas the theoretical approach aims to model and predict their magnetic behavior from first principles or phenomenological descriptions \cite{MuMaxReview2021}.


From the experimental perspective, research begins with the \textit{sample preparation} of thin films, multilayers, or nanostructures that define the system's geometry and magnetic anisotropy \cite{Fert2022ThinFilms}. These samples are then characterized through advanced \textit{magnetic imaging techniques} such as Lorentz TEM, MFM, and Kerr microscopy, which reveal the domain patterns at nanometer resolution \cite{Zhang2024MFM}. The resulting data are complemented by \textit{acquisition and analysis} of external stimuli, including magnetic and electric fields (STT and SOT effects), strain fields, and temperature variations, which allow for the control and manipulation of domain structures \cite{Finco2024Stimuli}. However, experimental images often contain noise, contrast distortions, and limited resolution, making quantitative parameter extraction challenging \cite{Phark2022ResolutionLimits}. As a result, bridging the gap between simulated and observed magnetic configurations remains an open problem that hinders both predictive modeling and materials discovery \cite{Finco2024Stimuli}.


In parallel, the theoretical and computational approach seeks to describe these systems at different scales. At the \textit{atomistic level}, models such as the Heisenberg Hamiltonian and Monte Carlo simulations capture spin interactions with atomic precision \cite{Hinzke2022Atomistic}. Moving to larger scales, \textit{micromagnetic simulations} employ the LLG equation to model the continuous magnetization field, implemented through frameworks like MuMax3, OOMMF, and Spirit \cite{MuMaxReview2021}.

Together, these methodologies constitute a multi-scale, multi-modal ecosystem that bridges empirical observation and theoretical understanding, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:problem_statement}, providing the foundation for modern research in magnetic materials \cite{Gomez2023MultiscaleReview}.

Within this integrated context, the study of magnetic nanostructures can be framed around two complementary and interdependent challenges: the direct and the inverse magnetic domain problems, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:framework} \cite{Feng2024}.

The direct problem involves predicting or generating the magnetic domain configuration that emerges from a given set of parameters—exchange interaction, anisotropy, DMI, external magnetic field, and temperature. This process defines the forward mapping from the parameter space to the spatial magnetization distribution and is typically addressed using micromagnetic or atomistic simulations \cite{MuMaxReview2021}. However, such simulations are often computationally intractable for broad parameter exploration or for incorporating stochastic thermal and experimental effects \cite{Hinzke2022Atomistic}. In contrast, the inverse problem aims to infer the underlying physical parameters responsible for a given observed domain configuration, such as those obtained from MFM or SP-STM \cite{Zhang2024MFM}. This task is intrinsically ill-posed: multiple parameter combinations can reproduce visually similar textures, leading to degeneracy and uncertainty in parameter estimation \cite{Feng2024}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{Figures/Framework.pdf}
    \caption{Unified framework illustrating the direct and inverse magnetic domain problems. The direct problem (top path) maps physical parameters—exchange interaction, anisotropy, DMI, external field, and temperature—to magnetic domain configurations through energy minimization or simulation. The inverse problem (bottom path) infers parameters from observed domain textures obtained via experimental techniques such as MFM or SP-STM. Bridging both directions through cycle-consistency ensures physical coherence and enables explainable, data-driven modeling of nanoscale magnetic systems.}
    \label{fig:framework}
\end{figure}

Bridging these two directions—ensuring coherence between generation (direct mapping) and estimation (inverse mapping)—is essential for predictive materials discovery \cite{Feng2024}. A unified and physically consistent framework that can simultaneously learn both directions offers a pathway toward explainable, data-driven modeling of nanoscale magnetic phenomena \cite{Ryu2025}.

Overall, the study of magnetic nanodots is constrained by three fundamental challenges that motivate the development of hybrid approaches combining physical modeling and data-driven inference:

\subsection{Degeneracy of Magnetic States}


\vspace{0.5cm}

Theoretical and computational approaches based on Hamiltonian formulations have become the dominant framework for studying magnetic nanodots due to their ability to provide physically grounded predictions under controlled parameter conditions. Micromagnetic simulations and atomistic spin dynamics solve the LLG equation or minimize the magnetic Hamiltonian to predict equilibrium and dynamic states \cite{MuMaxReview2021}. This approach offers several critical advantages: it enables exploration of parameter regimes inaccessible to experiment, provides complete three-dimensional vector field information at arbitrary resolution, and allows systematic investigation of competing magnetic interactions—exchange, anisotropy, DMI, Zeeman coupling—without measurement-induced perturbations \cite{Hertel2022MicromagneticLimits}. Moreover, simulations can incorporate thermal fluctuations, boundary conditions, and time-dependent external fields with explicit physical control, making them indispensable for understanding emergent phenomena such as skyrmion nucleation, domain wall dynamics, and magnetization reversal pathways \cite{Hertel2023Thermal}.

However, despite their physical rigor, Hamiltonian-based simulations reveal a fundamental challenge: the degeneracy of magnetic states. Different combinations of parameters—such as exchange interaction, anisotropy, and DMI—can produce visually similar or energetically comparable magnetic patterns \cite{Feng2024}. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:degeneracy_problem}, spin configurations sampled across the KDM (a proxy for DMI strength) and temperature parameter space exhibit striking visual similarity across distinct parameter regimes. This degeneracy arises because the magnetic energy landscape contains multiple local minima corresponding to metastable spin textures—vortices, skyrmions, stripe domains—that can be stabilized under different parameter sets \cite{Du2022MetaStableSkyrmions}. Small compensations between competing interactions can preserve the overall energy balance: for instance, an increase in anisotropy may be offset by stronger exchange or weaker DMI, yielding morphologically indistinguishable configurations \cite{Liu2023InverseML}. The visual similarity of domain textures across this parameter space creates fundamental ambiguity in inverse estimation tasks, complicating the unique identification of physical parameters from observed configurations and limiting the predictive power of both simulation-based and data-driven workflows \cite{Park2024}.


\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{Figures/TvsKDM.png}
    \caption{Visual inspection of spin configurations over the KDM parameter space conditioned on temperature. Multiple parameter combinations produce visually similar magnetic textures, evidencing the degeneracy problem that complicates unique parameter identification from domain observations.}
    \label{fig:degeneracy_problem}
\end{figure}

In nanoscale magnetic systems, the relationship between the Hamiltonian parameters and the resulting domain configurations is highly nonlinear and often degenerate \cite{Feng2024}. This degeneracy arises because different combinations of exchange coupling (J), magnetic anisotropy (K), and Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya interaction (D) can produce equilibrium states with similar total energy and comparable spatial magnetization patterns \cite{DeepSPIN2023}. From a physical perspective, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:energy_landscape}, the energy landscape of the magnetic Hamiltonian contains multiple local minima corresponding to metastable spin textures—such as vortices, skyrmions, or labyrinthine domains—that can be stabilized under different parameter sets \cite{Du2022MetaStableSkyrmions}. In practice, this means that small compensations between parameters can preserve the overall balance of competing interactions: for example, an increase in anisotropy can be offset by a stronger exchange term or a weaker DMI, yielding morphologically indistinguishable configurations \cite{Liu2023InverseML}. Thermal fluctuations and boundary effects further blur these distinctions by allowing transitions between nearby minima, effectively broadening the range of parameter combinations that generate similar observable textures \cite{Hertel2023Thermal}. As a result, the mapping from physical parameters to domain patterns is non-injective, complicating the inverse identification of Hamiltonian parameters from experimental images and introducing intrinsic ambiguity in inverse modeling tasks \cite{Ryu2025}.

The energy surface exhibits numerous metastable states (vortices, skyrmions, stripe domains) accessible under different parameter combinations, illustrating the fundamental non-uniqueness of the inverse problem: distinct paths through parameter space can lead to energetically similar or morphologically indistinguishable spin textures.}

\subsection{Sim-to-Real Gap}\label{sec:sim2real}

The study of magnetic domain structures is fundamentally constrained by a persistent discrepancy between computational predictions and experimental observations—a phenomenon known as the sim-to-real gap \cite{Ryu2025}. Computational modeling, whether based on atomistic spin dynamics or continuum micromagnetics, provides idealized representations of magnetic configurations derived from well-defined Hamiltonians and controlled numerical environments \cite{Hertel2022MicromagneticLimits}. In contrast, experimental characterization techniques capture the physical reality of magnetic textures, but do so through indirect, technique-dependent measurements that are inevitably affected by instrumental artifacts, environmental perturbations, and the intrinsic limitations of each imaging modality \cite{Wiesendanger2023SPSTMReview}. This fundamental asymmetry introduces systematic biases that manifest as domain-specific characteristics in both simulated and experimental data: computational approaches tend to produce smooth, noise-free, and boundary-condition-constrained spin fields, while experimental images exhibit instrumental noise, resolution-dependent blurring, contrast distortions, and measurement-induced perturbations \cite{Feng2024}. The resulting discrepancy poses a critical challenge for data-driven frameworks trained predominantly on synthetic datasets but intended for deployment in experimental workflows, as learned representations may fail to generalize across the simulation-experiment divide \cite{Hilgers2025}.

On the experimental side, magnetic domain images are inherently indirect representations of the underlying magnetization field. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:experimental_domain_shift}, techniques such as MFM measure the stray magnetic field above the sample surface through tip-sample interactions, introducing convolution effects, tip-induced artifacts, and resolution-dependent blurring (typically 30–50 nm) that obscure the true spin structure \cite{Kramer2021MFMNoise}. SP-STM, despite offering atomic-scale resolution (~1 nm), is sensitive to electronic density of states and tunnel current asymmetries, which modulate the observed magnetic contrast in ways that depend on tip magnetization, bias voltage, and surface electronic structure; however, it requires ultra-high vacuum (UHV) and cryogenic conditions, making it operationally expensive (800–1500 USD/hour) and inaccessible for routine characterization \cite{Wiesendanger2023SPSTMReview}. Lorentz transmission electron microscopy (5–10 nm resolution) captures projected magnetic induction, suffering from defocus-dependent phase shifts, integration effects along the beam path, and complex sample preparation requirements \cite{Raabe2020ImagingLimits}. As summarized in Table~\ref{tab:experimental_costs_monetary}, each technique presents a distinct trade-off between spatial resolution, quantitative accuracy, operational complexity, and cost. Moreover, environmental factors—substrate roughness, thermal drift, electromagnetic interference—introduce pixel-level noise and spatial distortions that vary across measurement sessions and experimental setups \cite{Hanneken2022ResolutionLimit}. Collectively, these effects mean that experimental datasets are not direct snapshots of magnetic configurations, but rather domain-specific transformations shaped by the physical principles and technical limitations of each imaging modality.

On the computational side, numerical simulations introduce their own domain-specific characteristics. Atomistic spin dynamics operate on discrete lattices with fixed exchange interactions and often employ simplified anisotropy models or neglect long-range dipolar fields for computational tractability \cite{Evans2014Atomistic}. Micromagnetic solvers discretize the continuum magnetization field into finite elements or finite differences, smoothing out sub-mesh-scale features and introducing numerical diffusion through time-stepping schemes in LLG integration \cite{Hertel2022MicromagneticLimits}. Boundary conditions—periodic, open, or fixed-spin—impose artificial constraints that can suppress edge effects or nucleation phenomena relevant to finite-sized nanodots. Thermal fluctuations are either omitted entirely or approximated via stochastic fields whose amplitude and correlation properties depend on the chosen numerical protocol \cite{Hertel2023Thermal}. Consequently, even among simulated datasets, variations in discretization, solver algorithms, and physical approximations create distinct "computational domains" with systematically different textural features, contrast, and statistical properties compared to experimental counterparts \cite{Feng2024}. This simulation-induced domain bias is further amplified when models trained on one computational framework are applied to data generated by another, or when synthetic training data fail to capture the stochastic, defect-driven, and measurement-limited nature of real experimental observations \cite{Hilgers2025}.

\subsection{Lack of physic-based interpretability}
A persistent challenge in data-driven modeling of magnetic systems is the lack of in-
terpretability and physical traceability in the results. While modern machine learning
methods can efficiently approximate the complex mapping between Hamiltonian pa-
rameters and magnetic domain configurations, their internal representations are often
opaque, providing limited insight into the underlying physics that govern the observed
phenomena \cite{Park2024}. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:interpretability_challenge}, this opacity complicates the validation of learned relationships against es-
tablished physical laws, such as exchange symmetry, anisotropy effects, or DMI-induced
chirality \cite{Ryu2025}. Furthermore, when combined with noisy experimental data or degenerate
parameter spaces, black-box models may produce accurate reconstructions without
preserving causal or physically meaningful dependencies \cite{Liao2023}. Consequently, the absence
of interpretability not only limits the scientific reliability of such approaches but also
hinders their use in guiding material design and hypothesis generation \cite{Sanchez2023ExplainableDesign}. Bridging this
gap requires the integration of physics-informed constraints, explainable architectures,
and cycle-consistent frameworks that ensure alignment between learned representations
and physically observable quantities \cite{Sanchez2024CycleConsistentMaterials}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.45\textwidth]{Figures/Interpretability.png}
    \caption{Interpretability Challenges}
    \label{fig:interpretability_challenge}
\end{figure}

The three outlined challenges---parameter degeneracy, data degradation, and lack of interpretability---are deeply interconnected within the study of magnetic domain systems \cite{Feng2024}. Parameter degeneracy reflects the intrinsic non-uniqueness of the physical mapping between Hamiltonian parameters and magnetic configurations, producing multiple metastable states with similar energy and morphology \cite{Du2022MetaStableSkyrmions}. Data degradation, in turn, limits the quality and reliability of both experimental and simulated observations, as noise, instrumental artifacts, and numerical discretization blur the underlying spin structures \cite{Kramer2021MFMNoise}. Finally, the lack of interpretability in data-driven models prevents the extraction of causal or physically meaningful relationships from these imperfect datasets, reducing their scientific value and applicability \cite{Park2024}. Together, these issues define a complex landscape where conventional simulation-based approaches struggle with scalability and uncertainty, while purely data-driven models risk overfitting and opacity \cite{Ryu2025}. This convergence of physical ambiguity, informational degradation, and epistemic opacity motivates the development of hybrid, physics-informed frameworks capable of enforcing physical consistency while enabling explainable inference across the direct and inverse magnetic domain problems \cite{Sanchez2024CycleConsistentMaterials}.

\section{State of the art}

\subsection{Degeneracy of Magnetic States}

The state of the art has therefore evolved along two complementary lines of work: efforts to make the forward mapping more discriminative and physically grounded, and efforts to make the inverse mapping more expressive, uncertainty-aware, and physically constrained. What follows is a synthesis of the major developments, their strengths and limitations, and the structural gaps that motivate new approaches.

\paragraph{High-Fidelity Forward Simulations.}
Research on the direct problem has traditionally relied on high-fidelity micromagnetic or atomistic simulations capable of capturing the subtle competition between exchange, anisotropy, DMI, dipolar interactions, and applied fields. Tools such as MuMax3, OOMMF, and Spirit have enabled precise modeling of domain walls, skyrmions, vortices, and labyrinthine patterns under a wide range of physical conditions \cite{MuMaxReview2021}. These approaches are widely used in both micrometer-scale and nanoscale magnetism, particularly in the study of skyrmions, chiral domain walls, and confined spin textures in ultrathin films. When coupled with forward-rendering models that explicitly account for instrument response—such as the point-spread function of MFM, the tunneling convolution kernel in SP-STM, or noise induced by probe electronics—these simulators generate synthetic images that closely resemble their experimental counterparts \cite{Phark2022ResolutionLimits}. This integration has been essential in applications such as skyrmion detection, micromagnetic parameter inference, and interpretation of high-resolution imaging data.

A complementary approach to continuum micromagnetics is the use of classical Monte Carlo methods combined with Heisenberg-type Hamiltonians, particularly effective for modeling nanogranular magnetic structures. Agudelo Giraldo \cite{Agudelo2016Granular} developed a comprehensive framework for simulating nanogranular magnetic thin films, where virtual grains were generated following lognormal volume distributions consistent with experimental observations. The structural relaxation at grain boundaries was modeled using the Morse potential under Monte Carlo relaxation, while magnetic interactions were simulated via the classical Heisenberg model with cubic magnetocrystalline anisotropy. The Hamiltonian incorporated four key contributions: (i) nearest-neighbor exchange interaction with distance-dependent coupling, (ii) magnetocrystalline, surface, and grain-boundary anisotropy, (iii) external magnetic field influence, and (iv) dipolar interactions computed via a hybrid spin-spin and spin-cell approach with a cutoff radius. Disorder at grain boundaries was introduced through anisotropy vectors dependent on lattice deformations, and temperature-dependent anisotropy effects were explicitly accounted for. Magnetic state relaxation was performed using the Metropolis algorithm, and the code was parallelized to handle the computational cost, with samples divided into cells with shared memory access. This methodology successfully reproduced monodomain-to-multidomain transitions as a function of grain volume, film thickness, and boundary anisotropy parameters, revealing superparamagnetic effects and blocking temperatures in scenarios with strong interfacial anisotropy, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:monte_carlo_flow}. Such physically accurate models substantially reduce non-physical degeneracies arising from oversimplified imaging assumptions.

    \centering

The framework successfully reproduces monodomain-to-multidomain transitions, superparamagnetic effects, and blocking temperature phenomena in nanogranular thin films.}
    \label{fig:monte_carlo_flow}
\end{figure}

Nevertheless, these simulator-based approaches—whether continuum or atomistic—are computationally expensive and sensitive to numerical discretization, initialization conditions, and solver choices, all of which can artificially inflate the observed degeneracy. More importantly, even perfect numerical solvers cannot eliminate intrinsic degeneracy: distinct combinations of DMI, anisotropy, exchange, and dipolar strengths may legitimately yield nearly indistinguishable patterns. Thus, while forward simulations reveal the structure of degeneracy, they cannot resolve it.

\paragraph{Differentiable and Surrogate Models.}
To mitigate these limitations, recent work has turned to differentiable and surrogate forward models. Neural surrogates, variational approximators, and differentiable micromagnetic solvers offer orders-of-magnitude speedups and enable gradient-based calibration and end-to-end integration into machine-learning frameworks \cite{DeepSPIN2023}. A typical neural surrogate architecture (Figure~\ref{fig:neural_surrogate_flow}) processes input physical parameters through dense layers to create feature embeddings, which are then upsampled via transposed convolutions to generate predicted spin configurations. These methods have been applied in problems such as rapid skyrmion-profile generation, uncertainty-aware forward prediction, and coarse-to-fine parameter scanning. While these models enable exploration of high-dimensional parameter spaces that are intractable for brute-force simulations, their accuracy is fundamentally limited by the diversity and physical realism of the training set. As a result, surrogate models may hallucinate non-physical spin textures or fail to generalize outside the training manifold. Consequently, this research line—although actively growing—is not yet considered a mature or commonly adopted tool for nanoscale domain reconstruction, highlighting the field's lack of physics-faithful, constraint-preserving differentiable solvers.

    \caption{Neural surrogate model architecture for fast forward mapping from physical parameters to spin configurations. The network processes input parameters through dense layers to create feature embeddings, then upsamples via transposed convolutions to generate predicted magnetic domain images.}
    \label{fig:neural_surrogate_flow}
\end{figure} 


\paragraph{Physics-Informed Neural Networks.}
In parallel, physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) and hybrid modeling frameworks have gained traction by embedding the Landau–Lifshitz–Gilbert (LLG) equation or energetic constraints directly into the learning objective \cite{Karniadakis2021PINNs}. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:pinns_flow}, PINNs augment the standard neural surrogate architecture with physics-based loss terms that enforce the LLG equation at collocation points throughout the spatiotemporal domain. These approaches have been tested in applications such as reconstructing domain-wall dynamics, modeling low-dimensional magnetic textures, or enforcing physical plausibility in neural surrogate outputs. By injecting physical laws into the learning process, they help reduce the feasible set of solutions and thereby restrict degeneracy. However, PINNs for magnetic systems remain difficult to train due to the stiffness of the LLG equation, challenges in balancing data fidelity and physics terms, and poor scalability to realistic thin-film geometries and finite-temperature regimes. Their use in nanoscale magnetic domain reconstruction remains exploratory rather than widely adopted.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{Figures/pinns_flow.pdf}
    \caption{Physics-informed neural network (PINN) architecture incorporating Landau–Lifshitz–Gilbert equation constraints. The training objective combines data-driven loss with physics-based residuals evaluated at collocation points, enforcing both accuracy and physical consistency.}
    \label{fig:pinns_flow}
\end{figure}


The objective in using Deep Learning for the Forward Mapping (Surrogate Model) in micromagnetics is to construct a function that rapidly maps a low-dimensional input vector of Hamiltonian parameters to the resulting high-dimensional spin configuration image, bypassing slow micromagnetic simulations. Given a vector of $P$ physical parameters $\mathbf{y}_n \in \mathbb{R}^{P}$ (e.g., $D$, $K$, $J$, $H_{\text{ext}}$), the task is to predict the image $\mathbf{\hat{I}}_n \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times \tilde{W} \times C}$. The core of the standard Deep Learning approach is to construct a deterministic function $\mathbf{\hat{I}}_n = f_{\Theta}(\mathbf{y}_n)$, where $f_{\Theta} : \mathbb{R}^{P} \to \mathbb{R}^{H \times \tilde{W} \times C}$ is parameterized by a Deep Neural Network (DNN), such as a Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) or an up-sampling network (like a transposed CNN or U-Net variant). The architecture uses dense layers to process the input vector $\mathbf{y}_n$ into an initial feature map, followed by transposed convolutions ($\otimes^T$) to increase the spatial resolution:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:transposed_conv_feature}
\mathbf{F}_l = \sigma_l(\mathbf{W}_l \otimes^T \mathbf{F}_{l-1} + \mathbf{b}_l)
\end{equation}

The final layers project this embedding into the target image space, $\mathbf{\hat{I}}_n$. The trainable parameter set $\Theta$ is optimized by minimizing the expected loss between the ground-truth spin configuration $\mathbf{I}_n$ (from high-fidelity simulations) and the prediction $\mathbf{\hat{I}}_n$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:theta_optimization}
\Theta^{*} = \arg \min_{\Theta} \mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{I}_n, \mathbf{\hat{I}}_n | \Theta) : \forall n \in \mathbb{N}]
\end{equation}
where the common regression loss function $\mathcal{L}$ is the Mean Squared Error (MSE):
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mse_loss_surrogate}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}} = \frac{1}{N_{\text{data}} H \tilde{W} C} \sum_{n=1}^{N_{\text{data}}} || \mathbf{I}_n - \mathbf{\hat{I}}_n ||^2
\end{equation}
The critical flaw of this purely data-driven approach is its lack of physical guarantees and its inability to capture the inherent multimodality of magnetic states. Since it aims for a single deterministic output $\mathbf{\hat{I}}_n$ for a given input $\mathbf{y}_n$, it struggles to maintain physical consistency and may yield non-physical textures outside the training distribution, a fundamental problem in tackling degeneracy.

Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) resolve this by merging the data-driven loss with physical constraints. The key is to enforce that the predicted magnetization field $\mathbf{M}_{\text{NN}}$ (which corresponds to $\mathbf{\hat{I}}_n$) must satisfy the Landau--Lifshitz--Gilbert (LLG) equation, which governs the dynamics. The LLG equation is incorporated through its residual $\mathbf{R}_{\text{LLG}}(\mathbf{x}, t)$, where the necessary spatial and temporal derivatives are calculated using automatic differentiation on the NN's output:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:llg_residual}
\mathbf{R}_{\text{LLG}}(\mathbf{x}, t) = \frac{\partial \mathbf{M}}{\partial t} + \frac{\gamma}{1+\alpha^2} (\mathbf{M} \times \mathbf{H}_{\text{eff}}) + \frac{\gamma \alpha}{1+\alpha^2} \left( \mathbf{M} \times (\mathbf{M} \times \mathbf{H}_{\text{eff}}) \right) = \mathbf{0}
\end{equation}
The PINN modifies the optimization objective by adding a Physics Loss ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{physics}}$) and a Magnitude Constraint Loss ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{magnitude}}$) to the total function:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pinn_total_loss}
\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{data}} + \lambda_{\text{physics}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{physics}} + \lambda_{\text{magnitude}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{magnitude}}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{physics}}$ penalizes deviations from the LLG equation across collocation points $\mathcal{P}$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:physics_loss_pinn}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{physics}} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|} \sum_{(\mathbf{x}_i, t_i) \in \mathcal{P}} || \mathbf{R}_{\text{LLG}}(\mathbf{x}_i, t_i) ||^2
\end{equation}
And $\mathcal{L}_{\text{magnitude}}$ enforces that the magnitude remains constant ($||\mathbf{M}|| = M_s$):
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:magnitude_loss_pinn}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{magnitude}} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|} \sum_{(\mathbf{x}_i, t_i) \in \mathcal{P}} \left( || \mathbf{M}_{\text{NN}}(\mathbf{x}_i, t_i) ||^2 - M_s^2 \right)^2
\end{equation}
By minimizing this physics-informed loss, the network is constrained to produce outputs that are not only statistically accurate but also physically plausible, thereby reducing the non-physical solution space and offering a more robust approach to mitigating degeneracy.




A qualitatively different perspective has emerged with the use of conditional generative models—including conditional VAEs, conditional GANs, and more recently diffusion-based conditional generators. These models treat the forward mapping as a conditional probability distribution instead of a deterministic function, enabling explicit representation of multimodality \cite{Ronne2024}. Figure~\ref{fig:cvae_baseline} illustrates a representative conditional VAE architecture for magnetic domain generation. Such techniques have been used primarily in large-scale imaging tasks, inverse scattering, and materials microstructure synthesis, with only very recent work beginning to apply them to magnetic textures, particularly in simulating ensembles of metastable states or producing uncertainty-aware synthetic datasets. Their ability to sample multiple plausible outputs for a given parameter set directly reflects the inherent degeneracy of magnetic states. However, generative models lack physical guarantees unless combined with physics-based constraints or energy-informed discriminators, and their outputs may deviate significantly from micromagnetic ground truth. To date, they remain promising but immature tools within nanoscale magnetism, with substantial gaps in validation, benchmarking, and physical consistency.

On the inverse side, early approaches framed parameter estimation as a supervised
regression problem, typically using convolutional or fully connected neural networks
trained on large synthetic datasets \cite{Feng2024}. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:supervised_regression}, these models are
computationally efficient and capable of predicting parameters in real time, making
them attractive for high-throughput applications in micromagnetism, spintronics, and
magnetic materials characterization. However, strict regression collapses the inherent
ambiguity of the inverse problem into a single point estimate, making predictions highly
sensitive to sim-to-exp mismatch and dataset bias. When two or more parameter regimes
produce nearly indistinguishable images, these models tend to yield unstable or
misleading predictions. Their dependence on large representative datasets introduces a
significant bottleneck, as the quality and diversity of synthetic training data are limited
by the accuracy, coverage, and numerical stability of the forward simulator.

    \centering

    \label{fig:supervised_regression}
\end{figure}


The limitations of deterministic regression have motivated a transition toward Bayesian
inference methodologies, which represent the inverse mapping as a posterior
distribution instead of a single estimate \cite{Hu2025}. Figure~\ref{fig:bayesian_inference} illustrates the Bayesian framework for parameter estimation. By explicitly modeling uncertainty and
multi-modality, Bayesian methods reveal the underlying degeneracy rather than hiding
it, offering richer insights into confidence levels, plausible parameter regions, and
non-identifiability. These frameworks have gained traction in the analysis of
magnetization textures, phase boundary detection, and materials discovery.
Nevertheless, Bayesian inference remains computationally expensive because it
requires repeated forward evaluations—each often involving a full micromagnetic
simulation or a surrogate model. Posterior accuracy also depends critically on the form
of the likelihood and prior, which are difficult to specify for systems governed by the
Landau–Lifshitz–Gilbert equation and subject to complex magnetic interactions.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.73\textwidth]{Figures/Bayesian.png}
    \caption{Bayesian inference approach for inverse parameter estimation. Instead of point estimates, Bayesian methods compute posterior distributions over parameters, explicitly representing uncertainty and multimodality inherent in degenerate magnetic systems.}
    \label{fig:bayesian_inference}
\end{figure}

To address these challenges, cycle-consistent frameworks have recently emerged as a
promising direction. These approaches couple a forward surrogate with an inverse
estimator, enforcing that both are consistent with one another through a cyclic
constraint \cite{Sanchez2024CycleConsistentMaterials}. By requiring
that inferred parameters can regenerate the original image via the forward model, these
methods naturally filter out many non-physical or inconsistent solutions. They have
shown potential in magnetic imaging analysis, ferromagnetic resonance inversion, and
domain morphology interpretation. However, cycle-consistent schemes inherit all
biases from the forward surrogate; if the surrogate is inaccurate or poorly calibrated,
the cycle constraint may amplify—rather than mitigate—systematic errors. Moreover,
their stability when applied to micromagnetic data remains largely unexplored, and
training can become unstable in strongly degenerate regimes.

Hybrid inverse methods represent a more recent development, combining statistical
inference with physics-informed constraints. These include Bayesian Physics-Informed
Neural Networks (PINNs), energy-regularized inverse networks, and approaches that
penalize violations of the LLG equation or magnetic free-energy minimization
\cite{Ryu2025}. By embedding physical priors directly in
the inference process, these methods restrict predictions to physically interpretable and
energetically plausible regions of parameter space. Such strategies have demonstrated
promise in nanoscale magnetometry, skyrmion parameter identification, and
magnetization-dynamics inversion. Their main drawbacks lie in computational cost,
training stiffness, and the difficulty of sampling complex posteriors that remain highly
multimodal in degenerate regimes. Table~\ref{tab:sota_inverse_magnetism} provides a comprehensive comparison of these inverse modeling approaches.

\subsection{Sim-to-Real Gap}

As described in the problem statement (Section~\ref{sec:sim2real}), the sim-to-real gap represents a fundamental challenge in applying data-driven models trained on simulated magnetic domain data to experimental observations \cite{Hilgers2025}. The systematic distribution shifts between computational predictions and experimental measurements—arising from idealized physics in simulations versus instrumental artifacts, noise, and measurement-specific transformations in experiments—cause learned models to fail when deployed in real-world scenarios \cite{Feng2024}. This section reviews the state of the art in domain adaptation techniques that have been developed to bridge the simulation-experiment divide, with particular emphasis on methods applicable to magnetic domain imaging and related materials characterization problems. The literature on domain adaptation has matured significantly over the past decade, encompassing data-level transformations, feature-level alignment, model-level transfer, and hybrid approaches that combine multiple strategies \cite{Wilson2020Survey}. While originally developed for computer vision tasks (object recognition, semantic segmentation), these methods have shown promise in scientific imaging domains where synthetic training data is abundant but real experimental data is scarce and expensive \cite{Sim2RealReview2021}.

\paragraph{Domain adaptation: overview and categorization.}
Bridging the simulation–experiment gap is commonly addressed through domain adaptation and transfer learning. Domain adaptation techniques enable models trained on source domains (typically simulated data) to generalize effectively to target domains (experimental observations) despite systematic distribution shifts \cite{Wilson2020Survey}. Figure~\ref{fig:da_overview} illustrates the fundamental concept: transferring knowledge from a data-rich source domain to a data-scarce target domain despite their distribution gap. These methods can be broadly categorized into several complementary approaches, each addressing different aspects of the domain shift problem.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{Figures/domain_adaptation_overview.pdf}
    \caption{Domain adaptation overview: transferring knowledge from simulation (source domain) to experiment (target domain) despite systematic distribution shifts.}
    \label{fig:da_overview}
\end{figure}

\subparagraph{Data-Based Domain Adaptation.}
Data-based approaches modify the training data itself to match the target domain distribution through augmentation (rotation, scaling, noise injection), synthetic degradation mimicking experimental artifacts (blur, resolution reduction), and domain mixing strategies \cite{Shorten2019DataAugmentation}. While computationally efficient and requiring no architectural changes, these methods depend on accurate modeling of the real data distribution and may require extensive manual tuning \cite{Cubuk2019AutoAugment}.

\subparagraph{Instance-Based Domain Adaptation.}
Instance-based methods adapt at the sample level by reweighting training instances according to their importance for the target domain or generating pseudo-labels for unlabeled target data through self-training \cite{Grandvalet2005SemiSupervised}. These approaches often incorporate test-time adaptation mechanisms that fine-tune model parameters on-the-fly using target domain statistics \cite{Wang2021TentTTA}. While flexible and requiring no target labels, they depend on reliable confidence calibration and can be sensitive to large distribution shifts \cite{Garg2022UnifiedTTA}.

\subparagraph{Feature-Based Domain Adaptation.}
Feature-based methods align learned representations across domains by minimizing statistical discrepancies in intermediate feature spaces \cite{Sun2016DeepCORAL}. Common strategies include Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) matching feature moments, CORAL aligning second-order statistics, and Domain-Adversarial Neural Networks (DANN) learning domain-invariant features through adversarial training \cite{Ganin2016DomainAdversarial}. These approaches are theoretically grounded but require careful hyperparameter tuning and may inadvertently discard task-relevant discriminative features \cite{Zhao2019LearningTransferable}.

\subparagraph{Adversarial Domain Adaptation.}
Adversarial approaches explicitly train domain discriminators alongside task predictors, leveraging gradient reversal layers to encourage feature extractors to produce domain-confounding representations \cite{Tzeng2017Adversarial}. The feature extractor maximizes domain confusion while the discriminator attempts to distinguish source from target samples, creating a minimax game driving domain-invariant representation learning \cite{Goodfellow2014GAN}. While powerful and end-to-end trainable, these methods can suffer from training instability and sensitivity to hyperparameters \cite{Arjovsky2017Wasserstein}.

\subparagraph{Autoencoder-Based Domain Adaptation.}
Autoencoder-based methods learn shared latent representations by training encoders and decoders to reconstruct both source and target domain inputs from a common bottleneck \cite{Ghifary2016DeepReconstruction}. The reconstruction constraint encourages the latent space to capture domain-invariant structural information while discarding domain-specific noise \cite{Liu2016CoupledGAN}. Task predictors operate on these shared representations, enabling transfer without explicit domain labels. However, reconstruction objectives may preserve task-irrelevant information or lose discriminative features \cite{Hoffman2018CyCADA}.

\subparagraph{Model-Based Domain Adaptation.}
Model-based approaches leverage pre-trained models from source domains and adapt them to target domains through transfer learning, fine-tuning, or feature extraction \cite{Zhuang2020Comprehensive}. Common strategies include full fine-tuning of all network layers on limited target data, selective fine-tuning of task-specific layers while freezing feature extractors, and using pre-trained features as fixed representations with new task heads \cite{Kumar2022FineTuning}. These methods are particularly effective when domains share structural similarities and target data is scarce, but risk negative transfer if domains are too dissimilar \cite{Tan2018SurveyDL}.

\subparagraph{Hybrid Domain Adaptation.}
Hybrid approaches combine multiple adaptation strategies to leverage their complementary strengths and mitigate individual limitations \cite{Peng2019MomentMatching}. A typical hybrid framework might integrate data augmentation for source domain enrichment, feature alignment through MMD or CORAL, adversarial training for domain confusion, transfer learning for initialization, and instance weighting for sample importance \cite{Long2018ConditionalDA}. By operating at data, feature, and model levels simultaneously, hybrid methods achieve robust performance across diverse domain shifts, though at increased implementation complexity and computational cost \cite{Saito2019SemiSupervisedDA}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{Figures/domain_adaptation_taxonomy.pdf}
    \caption{Taxonomy of domain adaptation strategies showing the seven main approaches with their key characteristics and selection criteria based on data availability, domain shift magnitude, computational budget, and interpretability requirements.}
    \label{fig:da_taxonomy}
\end{figure}

The choice among these domain adaptation strategies depends on the specific characteristics of the sim-to-real gap in magnetic domain imaging: the availability of target domain data, the nature and magnitude of distribution shifts, computational constraints, and the interpretability requirements of the application \cite{Wilson2020Survey}. For magnetic nanodot systems, where experimental data is expensive and limited, while simulation data is abundant but systematically biased, hybrid approaches combining model-based transfer learning with feature-level alignment and data-level augmentation offer a promising path toward robust sim-to-real generalization \cite{Hilgers2025}.

\paragraph{Summary and research gaps.}
Despite significant progress in domain adaptation methods, several challenges remain specifically relevant to magnetic domain imaging. First, most domain adaptation techniques assume smooth, continuous distribution shifts, whereas the simulation-experiment gap in magnetic imaging involves discrete instrumental modalities (MFM vs. SP-STM vs. Lorentz TEM) with fundamentally different physical measurement principles \cite{Raabe2020ImagingLimits}. Second, current methods often require access to unlabeled target domain data during training, which may not always be available for expensive experimental techniques \cite{Schlenhoff2022SPSTM}. Third, domain adaptation approaches typically focus on classification or regression tasks, with limited exploration of generative modeling for magnetic textures where both forward and inverse problems must be addressed simultaneously \cite{Park2024}. Finally, the interaction between domain adaptation and physics-informed constraints remains underexplored: how to preserve physical consistency (energy minimization, micromagnetic equilibrium) while adapting across domains is an open research question \cite{DeepSPIN2023}.


\subsection{Interpretability and Physical Traceability: State of the Art}

A persistent challenge in data-driven modeling of magnetic systems is the lack of interpretability and physical traceability in learned models. This limitation is not unique to magnetism: across computational physics and materials science, neural surrogates often match or surpass classical models in accuracy yet remain opaque, making it difficult to validate learned relationships against established physical laws or to extract causal insight \cite{Oviedo2022}. In nanoscale magnetism this opacity is particularly problematic, as inferred material parameters (exchange, anisotropy, DMI, external field, temperature) must be numerically precise and physically meaningful to support device design, hypothesis testing, and experimental planning.

Recent work (2020–2025) addressing interpretability in physics-informed learning can be grouped into four main directions: (1) post-hoc attribution and saliency methods adapted to physics data, (2) architectures aligned with Hamiltonian structure or physical operators, (3) cycle-consistent and energy-aware hybrid models that enforce forward--inverse coherence, and (4) probabilistic and disentangled latent models that expose uncertainty and factorized physical degrees of freedom. Below we summarize advances, strengths, limitations, and open gaps relevant to micromagnetic modeling.

\paragraph{Post-hoc attribution and local explainers.}
Classical explainability methods---such as saliency maps, occlusion tests, Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM), Integrated Gradients, and perturbation analyses---have been adapted to materials microstructure problems and magnetization textures \cite{Oviedo2022}. These tools highlight image regions most strongly influencing model predictions, enabling researchers to verify that regressors attend to physically meaningful structures (domain walls, skyrmion cores, vortex centers) rather than spurious artefacts or background noise (\cref{fig:gradcam_attribution}). Their advantages are simplicity, model-agnostic applicability, and utility as diagnostic screening tools. However, they provide only descriptive explanations rather than causal or physically grounded insight, and attribution maps can be unstable to small perturbations or architectural changes \cite{Li2023}.

Beyond gradient-based attribution, model-agnostic explainability frameworks such as SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) \cite{Lundberg2017SHAP} and LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations) \cite{Ribeiro2016LIME} have gained widespread adoption in machine learning applications. SHAP, grounded in cooperative game theory, assigns each input feature an importance value (Shapley value) representing its contribution to the prediction. LIME constructs local linear approximations around individual predictions by perturbing inputs and observing model responses. Both methods have been successfully applied to tabular data, text, and computer vision tasks in domains ranging from healthcare to finance \cite{Molnar2022Interpretable}. However, their application to continuous physical field data---such as magnetic domain images---faces fundamental conceptual limitations that constrain their utility in micromagnetic inverse problems (\cref{fig:shap_lime_concept}).

The core limitation arises from the independence assumption inherent in both SHAP and LIME: these methods treat each input feature (in the case of images, each pixel) as an independently perturbable entity whose contribution can be isolated and quantified \cite{Kumar2020Problems}. This assumption is reasonable for tabular datasets with genuinely independent features (e.g., patient age, blood pressure, cholesterol level) but becomes physically meaningless when applied to spatially continuous fields governed by differential equations. In magnetic domain images, pixel values represent discretized samples of continuous magnetization vector fields $\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{r})$ subject to exchange coupling, which enforces smoothness and correlation between neighboring spins. Perturbing or masking individual pixels---as required by SHAP and LIME---violates micromagnetic continuity, breaks exchange energy minimization, and produces configurations that cannot exist as equilibrium magnetic states \cite{Aharoni1996Introduction}. The resulting feature importance scores therefore reflect model sensitivities to non-physical perturbations rather than physically interpretable causal relationships (\cref{fig:shap_lime_limitations}).

Furthermore, SHAP's computational cost scales exponentially with the number of features, making exact computation intractable for high-resolution images (e.g., $128 \times 128$ pixels yield 16,384 features). Approximation methods such as Kernel SHAP rely on sampling strategies that may fail to capture long-range spatial correlations and collective magnetic structures (skyrmion lattices, stripe domains) whose physical significance emerges from coherent multi-pixel configurations rather than individual pixel contributions \cite{Covert2021Explaining}. LIME faces similar issues: its local linear surrogates are constructed by generating perturbed samples in a neighborhood around the prediction of interest, but defining meaningful neighborhoods for spatial field data is non-trivial, and random pixel perturbations do not respect the underlying physics \cite{Laugel2019Dangers}. For these reasons, while SHAP and LIME remain valuable diagnostic tools for identifying model behavior inconsistencies or dataset biases, they are insufficient for validating physical consistency or extracting domain-specific causal insight in continuous field inverse problems. Specialized methods that respect spatial structure (e.g., superpixel-based segmentation, physics-aware perturbations) or leverage domain knowledge (e.g., energy decompositions, latent space disentanglement) are required to achieve interpretability aligned with micromagnetic physics \cite{Achtibat2023Attribution}.

\paragraph{Architectures aligned with physics.}
A growing body of work embeds physical structure directly into model architectures. Examples include Hamiltonian- and energy-preserving networks, equivariant architectures for magnetic and electronic structure, operator-aware convolutions, and graph-based models for spin lattices \cite{Li2023}. Such models improve traceability by making internal components correspond to energy terms or symmetry representations; they enable per-term energy decomposition, sensitivity analysis, and physically meaningful extrapolation. Their main limitation is the need to predefine relevant physical terms and symmetries, which can reduce flexibility when unknown or emergent interactions are present.

\paragraph{Cycle-consistency, energy penalties, and hybrid forward--inverse training.}
Cycle-consistent frameworks pair a forward operator (parameters $\rightarrow$ texture) with an inverse model (texture $\rightarrow$ parameters) and enforce reconstruction and energy-based coherence \cite{Huang2023}. When the forward operator is a physics-informed simulator or a calibrated differentiable surrogate, cycle consistency acts as an interpretability constraint: predicted parameters must regenerate the input texture under the forward physics model. Additional energy penalties (differences in Hamiltonian energy between predicted and observed states) further restrict non-physical solutions. These methods have been used for uncertainty quantification and robust inversion in inverse-imaging and geophysical applications, and are beginning to be adapted to micromagnetic tasks. Their main weakness remains the fidelity of the forward module: surrogate bias can be propagated and reinforced.

\paragraph{Probabilistic models, uncertainty quantification, and disentangled latent spaces.}
Probabilistic generative models—normalizing flows, variational inference, and Bayesian neural networks—provide full posterior descriptions and explicit multimodality, making degeneracy and non-identifiability observable in the posterior landscape \cite{Wei2022}. Disentanglement methods (e.g., $\beta$-VAE variations) have been adapted to physical fields to align latent axes with generative factors, aiding interpretability and enabling targeted interventions in latent space \cite{Jacobsen2022}. Normalizing flows and flow-based variational methods have also been integrated with iterative solvers for inverse problems to produce calibrated posteriors and actionable uncertainty estimates \cite{Wei2022}. These probabilistic approaches are computationally demanding and require careful prior and likelihood modeling, but they offer the most principled route to exposing degeneracy. \section{Research Question}

The challenges outlined in the previous sections—parameter degeneracy, the sim-to-real gap, and lack of interpretability—converge to define a critical gap in the state of the art for nanoscale magnetic domain characterization. Current approaches struggle to simultaneously address the non-uniqueness of the inverse mapping, the systematic distribution shifts between simulated training data and experimental observations, and the opacity of data-driven models. This confluence of limitations motivates the central research question of this work:

\begin{center}
\textit{How can physics-informed deep learning frameworks be designed to simultaneously mitigate parameter degeneracy, bridge the sim-to-real gap through effective domain adaptation, and ensure physical interpretability in the forward and inverse modeling of magnetic domain states?}
\end{center}

This question is inherently multifaceted, requiring the integration of regularization strategies that respect the underlying Hamiltonian structure, domain adaptation methodologies capable of generalizing from synthetic to experimental data while preserving physically plausible solutions, and interpretability mechanisms that expose the relationship between learned representations and observable physical quantities. Addressing this research question requires moving beyond purely data-driven approaches toward hybrid frameworks that embed physical constraints, leverage domain adaptation techniques, and maintain computational tractability and generalization capacity across experimental and simulated magnetic imaging modalities.


\section{Objectives}

\subsection{General Objective}

Develop a physics-informed deep learning framework to simultaneously mitigate parameter degeneracy, bridge the sim-to-real gap through domain adaptation, and ensure physical interpretability in the forward and inverse modeling of magnetic domain states.

\subsection{Specific Objectives}

To achieve the general objective stated above, this work pursues three complementary specific objectives that tackle the core challenges of degeneracy, sim-to-real gap, and interpretability in the modeling of magnetic domain states:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Develop an image-based deep learning framework for parameter estimation from Hamiltonian-derived magnetic configurations, employing physically informed regularization to mitigate the effects of magnetic state degeneracy.

    \item Develop a regularized deep learning framework based on variational methodologies to generate consistent magnetic states from specified conditions, incorporating domain-aware training strategies to ensure validity across both synthetic and experimental imaging modalities.

    \item Develop interpretability methodologies for deep learning models based on post-hoc attribution methods to identify spatially relevant features associated with the underlying conditions of magnetic domain states.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Databases}\label{sec:databases}

This section describes the magnetic domain imaging datasets employed in this research, encompassing both synthetically generated training data and experimental reference datasets. The synthetic datasets provide controlled, physics-based ground truth for model development, while the experimental datasets enable validation of sim-to-real transfer capabilities and provide realistic imaging conditions for domain adaptation strategies.

\subsection{Hamiltonian-Based Synthetic Dataset Generation}\label{sec:synthetic_dataset}

The construction of the primary synthetic dataset is based on an atomistic Hamiltonian model that captures the main magnetic interactions governing spin configurations. This dataset serves as the foundation for training the physics-informed deep learning models described in Section~\ref{sec:Material_methods}.

\subsubsection{Hamiltonian Formulation}

The total energy of the system is described by:

\begin{equation}
H =
- \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{j \in nn} J \left( \mathbf{S}_i \cdot \mathbf{S}_j \right)
+ \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{j \in nn} \mathbf{D}_{ij} \cdot \left( \mathbf{S}_i \times \mathbf{S}_j \right)
- \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{ext}} \cdot \mathbf{S}_i
+ \sum_{i=1}^{N} K
\left(
\alpha_{i1}^2 \alpha_{i2}^2 +
\alpha_{i2}^2 \alpha_{i3}^2 +
\alpha_{i3}^2 \alpha_{i1}^2
\right).
\label{eq:H_full}
\end{equation}

This Hamiltonian incorporates four fundamental contributions, each governing a distinct physical mechanism:

\paragraph{Exchange interaction.}
\[
H_{\mathrm{ex}} = - \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{j \in nn} J \left( \mathbf{S}_i \cdot \mathbf{S}_j \right)
\]
The exchange constant $J$ controls the tendency of nearest-neighbor spins to align. Positive values favor ferromagnetic ordering, while negative values favor antiferromagnetic alignment. This term stabilizes smooth, low-energy magnetic domains.

\paragraph{Dzyaloshinskii--Moriya interaction (DMI).}
\[
H_{\mathrm{DMI}} =
\sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{j \in nn}
\mathbf{D}_{ij} \cdot \left( \mathbf{S}_i \times \mathbf{S}_j \right)
\]
The vector $\mathbf{D}_{ij}$ introduces chirality due to broken inversion symmetry, favoring non-collinear textures such as helices, Néel-type domain walls, and skyrmions.

\paragraph{Zeeman interaction.}
\[
H_{\mathrm{Z}} =
- \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{ext}} \cdot \mathbf{S}_i
\]
The external field $\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{ext}}$ couples to the spin orientations, tending to align the magnetization with the applied field direction.

\paragraph{Magnetocrystalline anisotropy.}
\[
H_{\mathrm{ani}} =
\sum_{i=1}^{N} K
\left(
\alpha_{i1}^2 \alpha_{i2}^2 +
\alpha_{i2}^2 \alpha_{i3}^2 +
\alpha_{i3}^2 \alpha_{i1}^2
\right)
\]
Here, $K$ denotes the anisotropy constant, and the components $\alpha_{ik}$ represent the direction cosines of the local spin orientation relative to the crystallographic axes. This term governs the preferred magnetization direction (easy or hard axes) and significantly influences domain morphology.

\vspace{0.3cm}
Together, these contributions enable exploration of a broad variety of magnetic textures, including vortices, stripes, labyrinth domains, helical states, and skyrmions.

\subsubsection{Simulation Procedure}

Spin configurations are generated using the Metropolis Monte Carlo method, which ensures statistical sampling according to Boltzmann probabilities. Systematic sweeps of temperature, exchange constants, anisotropy parameters, DMI strength, and external magnetic fields define the parameter space. Each simulation produces:

\begin{itemize}
    \item a geometry file describing lattice coordinates of the nanodot,
    \item multiple state files encoding the spin orientations for each parameter combination.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Image Construction Pipeline}

The raw spin states are transformed into image-based representations through a structured preprocessing pipeline:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Geometry parsing:} lattice coordinates are loaded and masked to enforce the nanodot shape.
    \item \textbf{Spin mapping:} the $S_z$ component is assigned to each lattice site to reconstruct magnetization fields.
    \item \textbf{Image rendering:} a red--blue colormap encodes opposite out-of-plane spin orientations on a fixed $39 \times 39$ grid.
    \item \textbf{Normalization:} pixel intensities are scaled to either $[-1,1]$ or $[0,1]$.
    \item \textbf{Dataset storage:} each entry is saved as a NumPy array containing the image and the corresponding Hamiltonian parameter vector.
\end{enumerate}

The resulting dataset contains tens of thousands of labeled images spanning a wide range of physical regimes, from fully ordered low-temperature states to disordered high-temperature configurations.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:Material_methods}

This section describes the methodological framework adopted to address the research objectives outlined in Section 5. The methodology integrates physics-informed deep learning architectures enhanced with cycle-consistent regularization, Bayesian hyperparameter optimization, domain adaptation strategies for sim-to-real transfer, and interpretability mechanisms for physical validation. The synthetic datasets described in Section~\ref{sec:databases} provide the controlled foundation for model development, while the experimental reference datasets guide domain adaptation and validation.

\subsection{Specific Objective 1: Degeneracy Mitigation in Forward and Inverse Problems}

This subsection addresses the first specific objective: developing a physics-informed deep learning framework that mitigates parameter degeneracy in both forward (parameter-to-configuration) and inverse (configuration-to-parameter) mapping tasks. The methodology integrates conditional variational autoencoders with physics-guided regularization, cycle-consistent constraints, and Bayesian hyperparameter optimization to ensure physically plausible and statistically robust magnetic state estimation.

\subsubsection{Physically Informed Conditional Variational Autoencoder}

A physically informed conditional variational autoencoder (cVAE) framework is adopted to address the degeneracy inherent to the forward problem, where multiple magnetic textures can arise from similar Hamiltonian parameters. The model learns a conditional distribution over domain configurations given a physical parameter vector $\mathbf{c}$, enforcing physical coherence through the latent structure and the learning objective.

The cVAE consists of an encoder $q_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x},\mathbf{c})$ producing a Gaussian latent embedding with mean $\mu_{\phi}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{c})$ and variance $\sigma^2_{\phi}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{c})$, and a decoder $p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{z},\mathbf{c})$ reconstructing the magnetic configuration. Conditioning is performed by mapping the Hamiltonian parameters into a physically informed latent prior $p_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{c})$ through a parametric network, following formulations used in physics-guided variational inference frameworks \cite{hermans2024cvai}. The complete architecture is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:pcvae_architecture}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.90\textwidth]{Figures/pcVae.png}
    \caption{\textbf{Conditional VAE architecture with physics-guided parameter conditioning.} The encoder $q_\phi$ maps magnetic configurations to a latent space, the decoder $p_\theta$ reconstructs them, and the conditional prior $p_\theta(z|c)$ structures the latent space according to Hamiltonian parameters.}
    \label{fig:pcvae_architecture}
\end{figure}

The total training objective is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cvae_total_loss}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}} 
= 
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rec}}
+ \beta \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KL}}
+ \gamma \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{phys}},
\end{equation}
where each component targets a specific aspect of physical or statistical consistency.

\paragraph{Conditional KL Divergence.}

The KL divergence regularizes the posterior distribution by enforcing proximity to a parameter-dependent latent prior:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:conditional_kl_divergence}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KL}}
=
D_{\mathrm{KL}}
\left(
q_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x},\mathbf{c})
\;\|\;
p_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{c})
\right).
\end{equation}

Unlike classical VAEs—which impose a fixed standard normal prior $p(\mathbf{z}) = \mathcal{N}(0, I)$—the conditional prior $p_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{c})$ introduces a physics-shaped structure into the latent space. This prior is implemented as:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:conditional_prior}
p_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{c}) 
=
\mathcal{N}
\big(
\mu_{\theta}(\mathbf{c}),\;
\Sigma_{\theta}(\mathbf{c})
\big),
\end{equation}
where $\mu_{\theta}$ and $\Sigma_{\theta}$ are neural networks that learn a latent distribution consistent with the Hamiltonian's parameter space.

This formulation prevents the model from collapsing physically distinct regimes into overlapping latent regions, reducing degeneracy by enforcing that different parameter vectors induce distinct latent priors. Consequently, the latent geometry reflects the topology of the physical system.

\paragraph{Physics-Guided Energy Penalties.}

The physics-based term $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{phys}}$ constrains the decoder to produce configurations consistent with low-energy magnetic states. Given the Hamiltonian:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:hamiltonian_decomposition}
H(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{c}) 
=
H_{\mathrm{ex}} + H_{\mathrm{DMI}} + H_{\mathrm{Z}} + H_{\mathrm{ani}},
\end{equation}
each component can be used to penalize deviations from expected energetics:
\begin{align}
H_{\mathrm{ex}} &= 
- J \sum_{i} \sum_{j \in nn} 
(\mathbf{S}_i \cdot \mathbf{S}_j), \\
H_{\mathrm{DMI}} &= 
\sum_{i} \sum_{j \in nn} 
\mathbf{D}_{ij} \cdot (\mathbf{S}_i \times \mathbf{S}_j), \\
H_{\mathrm{Z}} &= 
- \sum_{i} \mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{ext}} \cdot \mathbf{S}_i, \\
H_{\mathrm{ani}} &= 
\sum_{i} 
K (\alpha_{i1}^2 \alpha_{i2}^2 + \alpha_{i2}^2 \alpha_{i3}^2 + \alpha_{i3}^2 \alpha_{i1}^2).
\end{align}

Once the model has been trained, inference proceeds by conditioning the generative process on the physical parameter vector associated with the target magnetic regime (Figure~\ref{fig:cvae_inference}). The parameter vector is first processed through a multilayer perceptron (MLP) that constructs the conditional latent prior distribution. A latent sample is then drawn by combining the prior mean and variance with a stochastic perturbation sampled from a standard normal distribution, producing the latent representation used during generation. This latent code is subsequently merged with a secondary conditioning network that injects the physical parameters into the generative pathway, ensuring consistency with the Hamiltonian-defined regime. Finally, the decoder maps the conditioned latent vector into a magnetic domain configuration, yielding an image that is statistically coherent, physically plausible, and aligned with the specified material and interaction parameters. This prediction-stage pipeline enables the model to infer nanoscale magnetic states directly from physical conditions while preserving physical regularity learned during training.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.90\textwidth]{Figures/Inference.png}
    \caption{\textbf{Prediction phase in the conditional VAE.} The physical parameters are processed to construct the conditional prior, from which a latent sample is drawn and decoded into a magnetic configuration.}
    \label{fig:cvae_inference}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Cycle-Consistent Forward--Inverse Framework}

To reduce parameter degeneracy and reinforce physical identifiability, a cycle-consistent framework is incorporated on top of the physically informed conditional generative model. The core idea is to exploit the structure learned by the forward generator to regularize the inverse mapping, enforcing that parameter estimates remain consistent with the physical conditions that originally produced each magnetic configuration.

\paragraph{Forward Model.}
The forward model corresponds to the conditional generator $G_{\theta}$ already trained through the physically informed cVAE framework. Given a Hamiltonian parameter vector $\mathbf{p}$, the generator produces a corresponding magnetic configuration:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:forward_model}
\hat{\mathbf{x}} = G_{\theta}(\mathbf{p}, \mathbf{z}),
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{z}$ is sampled from the conditional latent prior. The generator has already been optimized using:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:forward_loss}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{forward}} 
= \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rec}}
+ \beta \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KL}}
+ \gamma \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{phys}}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Inverse Model.}
A complementary inverse estimator $F_{\phi}$ is introduced to recover the Hamiltonian parameters from generated or real images:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:inverse_model}
\hat{\mathbf{p}} = F_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}).
\end{equation}
The inverse model is trained with a supervised regression loss over the synthetic dataset:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:inverse_loss}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{inv}} = 
\left\| F_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}) - \mathbf{p} \right\|_{2}^{2}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Cycle Consistency Constraint.}
The forward and inverse models are coupled through cycle consistency. The cycle ensures that parameters reconstructed after a forward--inverse pass remain close to the original ones:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cycle_flow}
\mathbf{p} \xrightarrow{G_{\theta}} \hat{\mathbf{x}} 
\xrightarrow{F_{\phi}} \hat{\mathbf{p}}.
\end{equation}

The cycle-consistency penalty is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cycle_consistency}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cycle}}
= \left\| F_{\phi}\left( G_{\theta}(\mathbf{p}, \mathbf{z}) \right) - \mathbf{p} \right\|_{2}^{2}.
\end{equation}

This term forces the inverse model to be consistent with the learned physics of the generator and simultaneously restricts the generator to produce parameter-identifiable magnetic states, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:cycle_consistency}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.90\textwidth]{Figures/Cycle.png}
    \caption{\textbf{Cycle-consistency constraint.} Parameters are mapped to images via the forward generator $G_\theta$, then reconstructed via the inverse estimator $F_\phi$, enforcing round-trip consistency.}
    \label{fig:cycle_consistency}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Closed-Loop Joint Objective.}

During joint training, the generator and the inverse estimator are optimized under a combined loss:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:joint_loss}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{joint}}
= \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{forward}}
+ \lambda_{\mathrm{inv}} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{inv}}
+ \lambda_{\mathrm{cycle}} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cycle}}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Interpretation of Each Term.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{forward}}$ enforces physical plausibility and accurate magnetic configuration synthesis.
    \item $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{inv}}$ trains the inverse model to regress Hamiltonian parameters from images.
    \item $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cycle}}$ enforces round-trip (parameter $\rightarrow$ image $\rightarrow$ parameter) consistency.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Training Strategy.}
Training proceeds in two stages:

\paragraph{Stage 1: Pretraining the Forward Model.}
Only the cVAE-based generator is trained using the physically informed forward loss. This stabilizes the latent space and ensures physically coherent sampling.

\paragraph{Stage 2: Cycle-Consistent Joint Training.}
The forward generator $G_{\theta}$ and the inverse estimator $F_{\phi}$ are co-optimized. The update rules are:
\begin{align}
\theta &\leftarrow \theta - \eta_{\theta} 
\nabla_{\theta} \left(
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{forward}}
+ \lambda_{\mathrm{cycle}} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cycle}}
\right), \\
\phi &\leftarrow \phi - \eta_{\phi}
\nabla_{\phi} \left(
\lambda_{\mathrm{inv}} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{inv}} 
+ \lambda_{\mathrm{cycle}} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cycle}}
\right).
\end{align}

Note that the forward loss does not directly update the inverse model, whereas cycle loss updates both networks simultaneously, enforcing bidirectional consistency.

\paragraph{Hyperparameters of the Full Framework.}
The complete system introduces several interpretable hyperparameters:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\beta$: KL divergence weight (latent regularization).
    \item $\gamma$: physical-energy penalty weight.
    \item $\lambda_{\mathrm{inv}}$: strength of the inverse-regression term.
    \item $\lambda_{\mathrm{cycle}}$: strength of the cycle-consistency constraint.
    \item $\eta_{\theta}$, $\eta_{\phi}$: learning rates of forward and inverse models.
\end{itemize}

These hyperparameters govern the trade-offs between reconstruction fidelity, latent regularization, physical consistency, inverse accuracy, and closed-loop identifiability. Proper selection enables the framework to perform robust forward synthesis and stable parameter inference despite degeneracy in the image space.



\subsubsection{Bayesian Hyperparameter Optimization}

The full training pipeline introduces several interacting hyperparameters
that govern regularization strength, latent dimensionality, cycle constraints, and learning dynamics.
Because the loss landscape is highly non-convex and the model exhibits multimodal behaviors arising from magnetic degeneracy, a principled hyperparameter search strategy is required.
To this end, Bayesian hyperparameter optimization is employed to systematically identify the optimal configuration
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:hyperparameter_objective}
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\star} =
\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\lambda} \in \Lambda}
\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{J}$ denotes the validation objective (e.g., a weighted combination of reconstruction error, inverse-parameter accuracy, and physical energy consistency).

\paragraph{Surrogate Modeling of the Objective.}
Instead of directly evaluating $\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})$ for every configuration, Bayesian optimization constructs a probabilistic surrogate model $M$—typically a Gaussian Process (GP)—to approximate the unknown objective function.  
Given a set of observed evaluations 
$\mathcal{D}_{t} = \{ (\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{i}, y_{i}) \}_{i=1}^{t}$,
the GP surrogate models the objective as:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gaussian_process_surrogate}
\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}) \sim 
\mathcal{GP}\big( m(\boldsymbol{\lambda}),\, 
k(\boldsymbol{\lambda}, \boldsymbol{\lambda}') \big),
\end{equation}
where $m$ is the mean function (often taken as zero) and $k$ is a covariance kernel (e.g., Matérn or squared exponential).  
The GP posterior provides both a predicted mean $\mu_{t}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})$ and uncertainty $\sigma_{t}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})$, enabling guided exploration.

\paragraph{Acquisition Function.}
Bayesian optimization selects the next hyperparameter candidate by maximizing an acquisition function $\alpha(\boldsymbol{\lambda}; \mathcal{D}_{t})$ that balances exploration and exploitation.  
A commonly used acquisition function is *Expected Improvement* (EI), defined as:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:expected_improvement}
\alpha_{\mathrm{EI}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
= \mathbb{E}\big[ \max(0,\, \mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{best}} - \mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}) ) \big],
\end{equation}
which has the closed-form expression:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:expected_improvement_closed_form}
\alpha_{\mathrm{EI}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}) 
= (\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{best}} - \mu_{t}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})) 
\Phi(Z) + \sigma_{t}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}) \phi(Z),
\qquad
Z = \frac{\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{best}} - \mu_{t}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})}{\sigma_{t}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})},
\end{equation}
where $\Phi$ and $\phi$ denote the standard normal CDF and PDF respectively.

This acquisition function assigns high values to regions where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the GP predicts low objective values (\textit{exploitation}), or
    \item the model uncertainty $\sigma_{t}$ is high (\textit{exploration}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Sequential Optimization Loop.}
At each iteration, the Bayesian optimization loop performs:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Update the GP surrogate using all observed evaluations.
    \item Select the next hyperparameter configuration:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:next_hyperparameter}
    \boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t+1}
    = \arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\lambda} \in \Lambda}
    \alpha(\boldsymbol{\lambda}; \mathcal{D}_{t}).
    \end{equation}
    \item Train the forward--inverse system using $\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t+1}$ and compute the validation score:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:validation_score}
    y_{t+1} = \mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t+1}).
    \end{equation}
    \item Expand the dataset:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:dataset_expansion}
    \mathcal{D}_{t+1} = \mathcal{D}_{t} \cup \{ (\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t+1}, y_{t+1}) \}.
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

The process continues until convergence or until a fixed evaluation budget is exhausted.
The final estimate of the optimal hyperparameters is:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:optimal_hyperparameters}
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\star}
= \arg\min_{(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{i},\, y_{i}) \in \mathcal{D}_{T}}
y_{i}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Specific Objective 2: Sim-to-Real Domain Adaptation}

This subsection addresses the second specific objective: incorporating domain adaptation strategies to bridge the gap between synthetic micromagnetic simulations and experimental imaging data. The methodology adapts the cycle-consistent framework to account for domain shift, ensuring robust generalization from idealized simulations to realistic experimental conditions while maintaining uncertainty quantification.

\subsubsection{Domain Adaptation for Sim-to-Real Generalization}

As established in Section~\ref{sec:sim2real}, the systematic distribution shift between simulated training data and experimental observations represents a fundamental barrier to deploying learned models in real-world magnetic domain characterization. To address this challenge, we will explore domain adaptation strategies that bridge the gap between idealized simulations and experimental measurements, while maintaining the physics-informed cycle-consistent architecture described previously.

The experimental reference datasets described in Section~\ref{sec:experimental_datasets}---including the Leeds Skyrmion Dataset (STM imaging, ~1,200 images) \cite{LeedsSkyrmions2020}, Co/Pd MFM Dataset (MFM imaging, ~450 images) \cite{CoPdMFM2018}, and KIT 4D-STEM Magnetic Field Dataset (quantitative vector field reconstructions) \cite{KIT4DSTEM2021}---will serve as references for adapting our simulation-trained models to realistic imaging conditions. These datasets capture characteristic experimental signatures including measurement noise, instrumental artifacts, finite spatial resolution, and physical imperfections that are absent from idealized simulations, providing essential benchmarks for validating sim-to-real transfer capabilities.

\paragraph{Domain Adaptation Approach.}

The domain adaptation strategy will combine data-level augmentation and feature-level alignment techniques to enable sim-to-real transfer. The approach will be integrated into the cycle-consistent forward-inverse framework while preserving physics-informed constraints.

\paragraph{Data-Based Domain Adaptation.}
To reduce the distribution gap between simulated and experimental images, we will explore data augmentation strategies that introduce realistic variations to the synthetic training data \cite{Tobin2017DomainRandomization}. These may include noise injection, blur effects, contrast variations, and other transformations that mimic experimental imaging conditions. The goal is to create a more robust training distribution that better approximates the characteristics of real measurements without requiring large amounts of labeled experimental data.

\paragraph{Feature-Level Alignment.}
Beyond data augmentation, we will investigate methods for aligning the learned feature representations between simulated and experimental domains. Rather than operating at the pixel level, these techniques would match statistical properties of the latent features extracted by the encoder network. Several alignment approaches exist in the domain adaptation literature, including covariance matching, distribution distance minimization, and adversarial alignment. The specific combination of techniques will be determined based on empirical validation with the available experimental datasets.

\paragraph{Integration with Cycle-Consistent Framework.}

The domain adaptation strategy will be integrated into the cycle-consistent forward-inverse architecture described previously. The training objective will be extended to include domain adaptation terms alongside the existing physics-informed constraints:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rec}} + \beta \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KL}} + \gamma \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{phys}} + \delta \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cycle}} + \lambda \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DA}},
\label{eq:da_total_loss}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DA}}$ represents the domain adaptation loss component (which may combine data-level and feature-level alignment strategies), and $\lambda$ controls its influence relative to other objectives.

During training, simulated images with known parameter labels will pass through the complete forward-inverse cycle with full supervision. Experimental images from the reference datasets can be used without labels to guide the learned representations toward realistic imaging characteristics, ensuring that the model generalizes beyond idealized simulation conditions.

\paragraph{Uncertainty Quantification.}

When applying the inverse model to experimental images, rather than producing deterministic point estimates, we will implement uncertainty quantification methods to output parameter regions that reflect both model uncertainty and the inherent ambiguities in experimental observations.

Techniques such as Monte Carlo Dropout \cite{Gal2016DropoutBayesian}, ensemble predictions \cite{Lakshminarayanan2017Ensembles}, or Bayesian inference approaches can provide distributions over predicted parameters rather than single values. For each Hamiltonian parameter (exchange, DMI, anisotropy, temperature), the output would consist of a mean estimate along with confidence or credible intervals that characterize the prediction uncertainty.

This probabilistic approach acknowledges that experimental images may be compatible with multiple plausible parameter configurations due to degeneracy, measurement noise, and the sim-to-real domain gap. The specific uncertainty quantification methodology will be selected based on computational feasibility and empirical calibration against validation data. The complete domain adaptation strategy integrating these components is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:domain_adapted_cycle}.

\subsection{Specific Objective 3: Interpretability and Physical Validation}

This subsection addresses the third specific objective: implementing interpretability mechanisms that enable physical validation and traceability of model predictions. The methodology integrates multiple complementary techniques—post-hoc attribution, regression activation maps, latent space visualization, and physics-guided structural constraints—to ensure that learned representations align with underlying micromagnetic principles.

\subsubsection{Interpretability and Validation Across Domains}

A central requirement in the proposed forward--inverse modeling framework is the
ability to interpret how the neural architectures---both the forward generator and
the inverse regressor---extract, represent, and utilize physical information from
magnetization domain images across different domains. To this end, we adopt a multi-layer interpretability
strategy combining post-hoc attribution tools, regression-oriented activation metrics,
latent-space structural analysis, and physics-guided constraints embedded directly
into the training losses. This ensemble provides a coherent explanation of both
local spatial relevance and global representational behavior, with particular emphasis on validating that domain adaptation does not compromise physical consistency.

\paragraph{Post-Hoc Spatial Attribution.}

Since the forward and inverse models rely on convolutional feature extractors,
we compute a suite of post-hoc attribution methods to identify the image regions
driving predictions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Grad-CAM} highlights parameter-specific activation patterns by
    projecting gradients onto the final convolutional layers.
    \item \textbf{Integrated Gradients (IG)} provides a global relevance score for
    each pixel by integrating the gradients along a continuous interpolation path.
    \item \textbf{Occlusion Sensitivity Maps} perturb localized patches of the input
    and quantify prediction variation, acting as a model-agnostic attribution baseline.
\end{itemize}

These tools offer qualitative insights regarding whether the models attend to
physically meaningful structures such as domain walls, vortex cores, or characteristic
magnetization textures.

\paragraph{Regression Activation Maps (RAMs).}

To complement visual inspection, we introduce a quantitative interpretability metric
based on \emph{Regression Activation Maps} (RAMs). Let $A(x)$ denote the activation
map extracted from a Grad-CAM-like procedure adapted for regression, associated with
a predicted parameter vector $\hat{p}$. We define a spatial alignment score between
activation intensity and absolute prediction error:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ram_score}
    \mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{RAM}} =
    \frac{\langle A(x),\, |p - \hat{p}| \rangle}
    {\|A(x)\|_2 \, \|p - \hat{p}\|_2},
\end{equation}
which yields a normalized correlation in $[-1,1]$. High scores indicate that the
model assigns large relevance to spatial regions contributing directly to prediction
errors, while low or negative scores reveal spurious or unstable attributions.

This metric enables objective comparison across architectures, training stages,
and loss configurations, particularly when evaluating the impact of physics-based
regularization.

\paragraph{UMAP Latent-Space Analysis.}

To examine the global structure of internal representations, we apply
\emph{Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection} (UMAP) to latent features
extracted from both the forward and inverse models:
\begin{itemize}
    \item In the \textbf{forward model}, we analyze whether variations in the latent
    code $z$ correspond smoothly to changes in physical parameters $(J, K, T)$.
    \item In the \textbf{inverse model}, UMAP visualizes how magnetic textures with
    similar parameter values cluster in the feature space learned by the encoder.
\end{itemize}

This reveals global patterns such as parameter identifiability, degeneracies in
magnetization textures, and the influence of physics constraints on representation
geometry (Figure~\ref{fig:interpretability_strategy}). Well-structured latent clusters indicate that the encoder and generator
learn consistent manifolds aligned with physical invariants.

\subsection{Proof of Concept: Conditional Variational Autoencoder with Physics-Guided Conditioning}

The proposed architecture, illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:cvae_architecture}, implements a learned conditional prior $p_\theta(z|c)$ that depends on physical parameters $c$ (temperature $T$ and the derived KDM parameter combining anisotropy and Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya interactions), rather than imposing a fixed standard normal prior as in classical VAEs. This design choice aims to structure the latent space according to the underlying Hamiltonian parameter space, thereby reducing ambiguities arising from parameter degeneracy.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{Figures/cVae.png}
    \caption{Conditional VAE architecture with physics-guided parameter conditioning. The encoder $Q_\phi$ maps input magnetic configurations $x$ to a latent distribution $q_\phi(z|x,c)$ conditioned on physical parameters $c$. The prior network $P_\psi$ learns a parameter-dependent latent prior $p_\psi(z|c)$, while the decoder $G_\theta$ reconstructs magnetic configurations from sampled latent codes and conditions.}
    \label{fig:cvae_architecture}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Loss Function Implementation}

For this proof of concept, we implemented a simplified loss function without explicit physical constraints, focusing on reconstruction fidelity and latent space regularization. The total loss $\mathcal{L}$ minimized during training is:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:poc_total_loss}
\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}(x, \hat{x}) + \beta \cdot D_{\text{KL}}\left(q_\phi(z|x,c) \,\|\, p_\psi(z|c)\right),
\end{equation}
where:

\paragraph{Reconstruction Loss.}
Mean Squared Error (MSE) measures pixel-to-pixel fidelity between the original and generated image:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reconstruction_loss}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \|x_i - \hat{x}_i\|^2.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Conditional KL Divergence.}
The KL divergence regularizes the posterior distribution by enforcing proximity to a parameter-dependent latent prior:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kl_loss}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{KL}} = D_{\text{KL}}\left(q_\phi(z|x,c) \,\|\, p_\psi(z|c)\right).
\end{equation}

Unlike classical VAEs, the conditional prior $p_\psi(z|c)$ introduces a physics-shaped structure into the latent space. Since both the encoder posterior $q_\phi(z|x,c) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2)$ and the prior $p_\psi(z|c) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_2, \sigma_2^2)$ are Gaussian, the KL divergence admits an analytical solution:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:analytical_kl_divergence}
D_{\text{KL}} = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{j=1}^{d} \left[ \log\frac{\sigma_{2,j}^2}{\sigma_{1,j}^2} + \frac{\sigma_{1,j}^2 + (\mu_{1,j} - \mu_{2,j})^2}{\sigma_{2,j}^2} - 1 \right],
\end{equation}
where $(\mu_1, \sigma_1)$ come from the encoder $q(z|x,c)$, $(\mu_2, \sigma_2)$ come from the prior network $p(z|c)$, and $d$ is the dimension of the latent space. The hyperparameter $\beta$ (Beta-VAE) controls the regularization weight, allowing a balance between reconstruction quality and latent space structure.

\paragraph{Identified Limitations and Future Refinements.}
Despite these encouraging results, this proof-of-concept implementation reveals critical methodological challenges that must be addressed before deployment:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hyperparameter Optimization}: The current architecture was trained with manually selected hyperparameters. To fully exploit the model's capacity, a systematic Bayesian optimization procedure (e.g., using Optuna~\cite{Optuna2022}) must be conducted to identify optimal values for $\beta$, latent dimensionality $d$, learning rates, and network depth. The baseline performance reported here likely underestimates the architecture's true potential.

    \item \textbf{Pixel-Level Metrics and Geometric Invariance}: A fundamental limitation emerged during evaluation: magnetic domain configurations are inherently invariant to spatial transformations (rotations, translations, and reflections). Two configurations that differ only by a rigid transformation are \textit{physically equivalent}, yet pixel-wise metrics such as MSE assign them high dissimilarity. This misalignment between the evaluation metric and the physical equivalence relation leads to systematically underestimated generative quality. Figure~\ref{fig:generation} qualitatively demonstrates that the model can produce physically coherent states, but quantitative assessment via MSE penalizes geometrically transformed yet physically valid outputs. Alternative evaluation strategies must be explored, including:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Rotation- and translation-invariant metrics} based on topological descriptors (e.g., skyrmion number, vorticity fields, or Betti numbers).
        \item \textit{Energy-based metrics} that assess the physical consistency of generated states through micromagnetic energy functionals rather than pixel-level comparisons.
        \item \textit{Structural similarity measures} (e.g., Fréchet Inception Distance adapted to magnetic textures) that capture perceptual and physical similarity beyond pixel alignment.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

These findings validate the feasibility of the proposed architecture and motivate the subsequent integration of physics-informed regularization to further reduce degeneracy, improve generalization to unseen parameter regimes, and ensure strict adherence to micromagnetic principles. Moreover, they highlight the necessity of developing evaluation protocols that respect the geometric symmetries inherent to magnetic domain structures.


\subsection{Proof of Concept: Inverse Problem for Parameter Estimation via Transfer Learning}

Having established a baseline for the generative modeling of magnetic domain states through the conditional VAE, we now address the \textit{inverse problem}: the task of estimating underlying physical parameters from observed magnetic configurations. This complementary direction is critical for the long-term objective of integrating forward (state generation) and inverse (parameter inference) workflows into a unified physics-informed framework. In this proof-of-concept, we explore the feasibility of multivariate regression from spin structure images to physical parameters using convolutional neural networks (CNNs) trained via transfer learning. Additionally, we introduce a novel passive interpretability method—\textit{Regression Activation Maps (RAMs)}—to identify which spatial regions of the input contribute most to accurate predictions.

\subsubsection{Problem Formulation and Architecture Design}

The inverse problem is formulated as a multivariate regression task, where the model $f_\theta: \mathbb{R}^{H \times W} \to \mathbb{R}^p$ maps an input image representing the material's spin structure to a vector of $p$ physical parameters. For the KDM DATABASE SPINERS, the target variables are temperature $T$ and the Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya interaction magnitude $KDM$. For the MATERIAL SPINNERS DATA, we predict $T$, $J_{ex2}$, $J_{ex3}$, and $J_{ex4}$.

The approach is based on \textit{transfer learning}, leveraging pretrained image encoders from state-of-the-art computer vision architectures to extract high-level representations from spin configurations before performing regression. Pretrained weights from ImageNet are employed, allowing the model to benefit from learned visual features and accelerate convergence. The final classification layers of the original architectures are replaced with regression-specific dense layers, enabling multivariate continuous output.

\paragraph{Training Details.}
All convolutional models were trained using the Adam optimizer with mean squared error (MSE) as the primary loss function:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mse_loss}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \left(y_i - \hat{y}_i\right)^2,
\end{equation}
and mean absolute error (MAE) as a complementary evaluation metric:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mae}
\text{MAE} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \left|y_i - \hat{y}_i\right|.
\end{equation}

The dataset was split into 80\% training, 10\% validation, and 10\% testing. Data normalization was performed using MinMaxScaler from scikit-learn. Models were trained for 10 epochs on an A100 GPU (Google Colab). Performance was evaluated using the coefficient of determination ($R^2$) and symmetric mean absolute percentage error (SMAPE):
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:r_squared}
R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \bar{y})^2},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:smape}
\text{SMAPE} = \frac{100}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{|y_i - \hat{y}_i|}{(|y_i| + |\hat{y}_i|)/2}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Discussion.}
This proof-of-concept demonstrates that transfer learning-based CNNs can effectively solve the inverse problem of parameter estimation from magnetic domain images. Key findings include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Architecture Selection}: DenseNet121 achieves the best balance across datasets and target variables, with particularly strong performance for temperature estimation.
    \item \textbf{Parameter-Specific Challenges}: KDM estimation is inherently more difficult than temperature estimation, particularly in high-temperature regimes where thermal disorder dominates.
    \item \textbf{Cross-Dataset Generalization}: The model trained on one Hamiltonian formulation can generalize to predict temperature in a different physical regime, suggesting that latent representations capture transferable magnetic features.
    \item \textbf{Interpretability}: UMAP analysis reveals that deeper layers progressively organize representations according to physical parameters, while RAMs provide spatial interpretability, highlighting which regions of the magnetic configuration contribute most to accurate predictions.
\end{itemize}

These results establish a baseline for inverse parameter estimation and motivate future integration with the forward generative pipeline (cVAE) into a unified bidirectional framework capable of both state generation and parameter inference.

\section{Conclusions} \label{sec:Conclusions}

This work proposes a physics-informed deep learning framework addressing parameter degeneracy, data quality degradation, and interpretability in nanoscale magnetic domain characterization. Proof-of-concept validation on over 200,000 Monte Carlo images demonstrates that conditional VAEs with learned physics-guided priors achieve high-fidelity reconstructions while organizing latent representations according to physical parameters, and that transfer learning-based CNNs (DenseNet121) effectively estimate Hamiltonian parameters with strong temperature prediction performance. The Regression Activation Maps methodology reveals adaptive attention to topologically relevant features, confirming interpretable feature learning aligned with physical structure.

Critical limitations guide future development: Bayesian hyperparameter optimization to exploit model capacity, rotation-invariant evaluation metrics incorporating topological descriptors and energy-based measures, and integration of forward-inverse pipelines into a unified cycle-consistent framework with micromagnetic energy penalties and LLG residuals. Domain adaptation strategies will bridge simulations to experimental microscopy, while the validated components establish a foundation demonstrating feasibility for addressing degeneracy and enabling interpretable, physics-consistent predictions in experimental deployment.


\section{Future Work}\label{sec:future_work}

Future research will consolidate this framework into a robust deployable system through the following directions:

\paragraph{Hyperparameter Optimization and Model Refinement.}
Bayesian optimization (e.g., Optuna) will systematically tune the $\beta$ parameter, latent dimensionality, learning rates, network depth, and prior capacity to enhance generative quality and parameter estimation accuracy.

\paragraph{Integration of Forward and Inverse Pipelines.}
Unifying the cVAE and DenseNet-based regressor into a cycle-consistent framework will enforce bidirectional constraints: generated configurations must yield consistent parameter estimates through the inverse model, and vice versa. Cycle-consistency regularization ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{cycle}}$) is expected to reduce parameter degeneracy, particularly for DMI in high-temperature regimes.

\paragraph{Physics-Informed Regularization.}
Incorporating micromagnetic energy functionals (exchange, anisotropy, Zeeman, DMI) and LLG residuals ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{energy}}$, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{LLG}}$) will ensure generated configurations are energetically stable and dynamically consistent, critical for generalization to unseen parameter regimes.

\paragraph{Domain Adaptation for Sim-to-Real Transfer.}
Bridging the sim-to-real gap requires empirical evaluation of data-level augmentation, feature-level alignment, and hybrid strategies using experimental datasets (Leeds skyrmions, Co/Pd MFM, KIT 4D-STEM). The interplay between domain adaptation and physics-informed constraints will be investigated to preserve physical consistency while adapting to experimental imaging conditions.

\paragraph{Evaluation Metrics and Interpretability.}
Rotation/translation-invariant topological descriptors (skyrmion number, vortex counting, domain wall density), energy-based metrics from micromagnetic Hamiltonians, and perceptual similarity measures will supplement pixel-level comparisons. The RAMs methodology will be extended and compared with other interpretability techniques across diverse magnetic regimes.

\paragraph{Comparative Evaluation with Alternative Architectures.}
Controlled comparisons between cVAEs, diffusion models, and transformer-based generators will evaluate trade-offs in generation quality, parameter efficiency, training stability, and interpretability, leveraging emerging architectures' potential for superior sample quality, mode coverage, and long-range dependency modeling.
