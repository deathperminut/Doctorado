\section{Abstract}

Magnetic domain structures in nanoscale materials play a central role in spintronics, biomedicine, and energy storage technologies. Magnetic nanoparticles and nanodots exhibit complex spin textures—vortices, skyrmions, and stripe domains—whose functional properties depend critically on Hamiltonian parameters such as exchange, anisotropy, Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya interaction, temperature, and external fields. Understanding and controlling these states requires establishing robust mappings between physical parameters and spatial spin configurations.

Three fundamental challenges make this an inherently ill-posed problem. First, the nonlinearity and high dimensionality of the parameter-to-configuration mapping render it non-injective: distinct parameter combinations relax into energetically comparable, visually similar magnetic textures, so unique parameters cannot be recovered by direct analytical inversion (degeneracy). Second, the accessible and low-cost experimental modalities—a single magnetic force microscopy or polar-Kerr image, or a single normal-incidence STXM scan—resolve only one scalar projection of the magnetization vector, typically the out-of-plane component $s_z$; recovering the full three-dimensional texture requires multi-acquisition protocols (vector-MOKE, tilt-series STXM tomography) that entail greater instrumental complexity and machine time and are not always feasible for a given material or setup. This single-axis observation discards information and aggravates the ill-conditioning: for instance, reversing the sign of the antisymmetric Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya interaction mirrors the in-plane chirality while leaving $s_z$ invariant, so configurations of opposite handedness become indistinguishable, and parameters that shape the in-plane structure rather than the $s_z$ contrast become weakly identifiable. Third, experimental data are scarce and costly to acquire, whereas synthetic data—although abundant and fully labelled—are idealized and diverge from experiment through instrumental noise, finite probe resolution, and tip–sample convolution, so models trained on simulations may fail to transfer, defining a sim-to-real gap.

Deep learning has shown promise in addressing these problems. The inverse problem of inferring physical parameters from magnetic images has been tackled with convolutional neural networks and explainable regression frameworks, while the forward problem of generating domain configurations from parameters has been explored using variational autoencoders and conditional generative adversarial networks. However, critical gaps remain: parameter degeneracy together with the partial observability of single-axis ($s_z$) measurements prevents robust inverse estimation, the sim-to-real gap between idealized synthetic data and noisy, scarce experimental images limits model reliability, and lack of physics-based interpretability obscures connections between learned representations and fundamental magnetic interactions. Existing generative approaches suffer from poor interpretability, instability under noise, and insufficient physical constraints.

This work proposes a unified, physics-informed deep learning framework that couples parameter estimation from magnetic images with conditional generative modelling to mitigate parameter degeneracy and characterize the chirality ambiguity intrinsic to single-axis observation, bridge the simulation-to-experiment gap, and ensure interpretability. Rather than conditioning generation on static parameters alone, the framework exploits how magnetic textures transition under controlled changes of ambient conditions—temperature and external field—using these trajectories as additional physical information to disambiguate parameter combinations that would otherwise yield near-identical static textures. Learning is guided by physically grounded objectives defined on magnetic observables rather than pixel-level similarity alone, together with domain-aware strategies that preserve validity across synthetic and experimental modalities. The supporting data-generation engine is reformulated algebraically and parallelized to accelerate simulation without altering the underlying physics, while post-hoc attribution methods reveal the spatial features driving each prediction. Finally, these components are integrated into a single, unified framework that autonomous agents can reuse.

This research addresses critical national and regional technological needs by advancing Colombia's capabilities in materials characterization and intelligent data processing. The framework supports development of next-generation magnetic memory and energy storage systems, identified as strategic innovation priorities. By integrating physics-informed deep learning with experimental microscopy workflows, this work bridges fundamental materials science and industrial spintronics applications. Furthermore, it aligns with the mission of the Digital Signal and Control Processing Group, extending the group's expertise in digital image processing to nanoscale materials characterization and fostering interdisciplinary collaboration between materials physics, machine learning, and computational imaging.

\section{Motivation}

%1.1. Contexto general: el magnetismo a nanoescala

The study of magnetic systems at the nanoscale has gained strategic importance in
modern materials science \cite{Wang2022}. In particular, magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs) and nanodots
constitute platforms where spin interactions and collective quantum properties give
rise to emergent phenomena of great scientific and technological interest \cite{Li2021SelfAssembly}. At these
scales, the balance between exchange interactions, anisotropy, DMI, and external magnetic fields generates a wide variety of magnetic
textures—vortices, skyrmions, helical domains, and labyrinthine configurations \cite{Fert2023}. \textit{Magnetic vortices} are characterized by an in-plane curling magnetization pattern with a nanometer-scale core where spins align perpendicular to the film plane, minimizing magnetostatic energy while providing topological stability. \textit{Skyrmions} are topologically protected whirl-like spin structures with non-zero skyrmion number, stabilized by the competition between exchange, DMI, and Zeeman interactions, exhibiting particle-like behavior and ultralow current-driven mobility ideal for racetrack memory. \textit{Helical domains} emerge from periodic modulation of magnetization driven by DMI in thin films, forming stripe-like patterns with alternating chirality that facilitate spin-wave propagation. \textit{Labyrinthine configurations} arise at intermediate anisotropy regimes, displaying complex branched domain wall networks that reflect competing energy contributions and metastable equilibria. These structures determine the magnetic response, thermal stability, and switching dynamics of the material, which are key aspects for functionality in advanced devices \cite{Bogdanov2020Skyrmions}.

%1.2. Relevancia aplicada: por qué las nanopartículas magnéticas importan

Magnetic NPs and nanodots possess a unique versatility that makes them relevant across multiple technological and scientific domains (Figure~\ref{fig:applications}). In the field of spintronics and non-volatile memory technologies, topologically protected magnetic textures—such as skyrmions and magnetic vortices—enable the encoding and manipulation of information as stable, switchable magnetic bits. These nanoscale spin configurations support the development of next-generation memory architectures including MRAM, racetrack memory devices, and reconfigurable magnetic logic gates. The appeal of these technologies lies in their combination of high storage density, ultralow power consumption, radiation hardness, and non-volatility, making them promising candidates for replacing conventional charge-based electronics in applications ranging from edge computing to space-grade systems \cite{Eurek2024}. Beyond information processing, the dynamic magnetic response of these nanostructures under applied fields or current-induced torques allows for fast and deterministic switching, essential for achieving competitive read/write speeds in practical memory devices.

In parallel, magnetic NPs have emerged as transformative agents in biomedical and energy-related applications, where their spin dynamics and domain structure directly govern functional performance. In biomedicine, magnetic NPs are employed in targeted oncological hyperthermia therapies, where alternating magnetic fields induce localized heating to selectively destroy tumor cells, as well as in advanced drug delivery systems that exploit magnetic guidance for site-specific therapeutic action \cite{MLDesign2023}. Their utility extends to contrast enhancement in MRI, where tailored magnetic anisotropy and relaxation properties improve diagnostic sensitivity. In the energy sector, magnetic NPs are investigated for magnetocaloric refrigeration—a sustainable alternative to vapor-compression cooling—thermoelectric energy conversion, and reversible magnetic energy storage systems \cite{He2025}. In all these contexts, the efficiency of the functional response is fundamentally determined by the internal magnetic domain structure, anisotropy landscape, and the mechanisms of energy transfer between spin, lattice, and thermal degrees of freedom \cite{Nehan2024}. This broad spectrum of applications underscores the critical need for robust characterization and predictive modeling frameworks capable of linking Hamiltonian parameters to experimentally observable magnetic configurations.

In recent years, the integration of AI with computational physics has opened new avenues for the modeling and prediction of complex material behaviors \cite{Huang2021AIMaterials}. Traditional simulation methods offer precise control over physical parameters but are limited by high computational cost and restricted scalability across parameter spaces \cite{Liao2023}. AI-driven approaches, particularly deep learning architectures, enable the extraction of latent physical relationships directly from data, accelerating the exploration of vast configuration spaces that would otherwise be computationally intractable \cite{Hilgers2025}. In the context of nanoscale magnetism, such hybrid frameworks bridge the gap between experimental observation and theoretical modeling, providing a data-efficient path toward inverse design and the discovery of emergent spin phenomena \cite{Liao2023}.

\section{Problem Statement}\label{sec:problem_statement}
%%%% Experimental and Theoretical Approaches to Magnetic Nanostructures
 
The study of magnetic nanostructures integrates two complementary approaches: the \textbf{experimental} and the \textbf{theoretical-simulation} pathways \cite{Andreu2020Anisotropic}. The experimental route focuses on the physical synthesis, visualization, and measurement of magnetic systems \cite{Nistico2020Magnetic}, whereas the theoretical approach aims to model and predict their magnetic behavior from first principles or phenomenological descriptions \cite{Li2023Magnetic}.


From the experimental perspective, research begins with the \textit{sample preparation} of thin films, multilayers, or nanostructures that define the system's geometry and magnetic anisotropy \cite{Fert2022ThinFilms}. These samples are then characterized through advanced \textit{magnetic imaging techniques} such as Lorentz TEM, MFM, and Kerr microscopy, which reveal the domain patterns at nanometer resolution \cite{Li2023Magnetic}. The resulting data are complemented by \textit{acquisition and analysis} of external stimuli, including magnetic and electric fields (STT and SOT effects), strain fields, and temperature variations, which allow for the control and manipulation of domain structures \cite{Shao2021SOT}. However, experimental images often contain noise, contrast distortions, and limited resolution, making quantitative parameter extraction challenging \cite{Nistico2020Magnetic}. Moreover, the most accessible and low-cost acquisitions—a single MFM or polar-Kerr image, or a single normal-incidence STXM scan—resolve only one scalar projection of the magnetization vector, typically its out-of-plane component $s_z$; recovering the full three-dimensional spin texture demands multi-acquisition protocols such as vector-MOKE or tilt-series tomography, which add instrumental complexity and machine time and are not always feasible for a given material or setup \cite{Li2023Magnetic}. Relying on a single projection discards part of the physical information: the in-plane degrees of freedom, and in particular the handedness fixed by the sign of the antisymmetric Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya coupling, leave the observed $s_z$ pattern unchanged. This partial observability, compounded by the scarcity and cost of experimental measurements, stands alongside noise and finite resolution as a core experimental limitation. As a result, bridging the gap between simulated and observed magnetic configurations remains an open problem that hinders both predictive modeling and materials discovery \cite{Raviolo2020Angular}.


In parallel, the theoretical and computational approach seeks to describe these systems at different scales. At the \textit{atomistic level}, models such as the Heisenberg Hamiltonian and Monte Carlo simulations capture spin interactions with atomic precision \cite{Chrobak2020Atomic}. Moving to larger scales, \textit{micromagnetic simulations} employ the LLG equation to model the continuous magnetization field, implemented through frameworks like MuMax3, OOMMF, and Spirit \cite{Li2023Magnetic}.

These simulation methods, however, are computationally demanding, and this cost becomes the limiting factor when generating the large, densely sampled datasets that data-driven approaches require. Reaching a thermodynamically relaxed configuration for a single parameter set entails a sequential Monte Carlo relaxation: thousands of sweeps over the lattice, each attempting a local spin update whose acceptance depends on the previous state, typically wrapped in a simulated-annealing schedule that slowly lowers the temperature to avoid metastable trapping \cite{Chrobak2020Atomic}. Because the Markov chain is inherently sequential and must be repeated over many stochastic replicas and across a high-dimensional grid of Hamiltonian parameters, the total cost grows rapidly, and conventional implementations---often relying on site-by-site loops in general-purpose scientific code---leave much of the available hardware parallelism unused \cite{Misiurev2024Modeling}. This computational bottleneck constrains how finely the parameter space can be explored and motivates reformulations of the sampling procedure that preserve the underlying physics while exploiting modern parallel hardware.

Together, these methodologies constitute a multi-scale, multi-modal ecosystem that bridges empirical observation and theoretical understanding, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:problem_statement}, providing the foundation for modern research in magnetic materials \cite{Misiurev2024Modeling}.

Within this integrated context, the study of magnetic nanostructures can be framed around two complementary and interdependent challenges: the direct and the inverse magnetic domain problems, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:framework} \cite{Feng2024}.

The direct problem involves predicting or generating the magnetic domain configuration that emerges from a given set of parameters—exchange interaction, anisotropy, DMI, external magnetic field, and temperature. This process defines the forward mapping from the parameter space to the spatial magnetization distribution and is typically addressed using micromagnetic or atomistic simulations \cite{Li2023Magnetic}. However, such simulations are often computationally intractable for broad parameter exploration or for incorporating stochastic thermal and experimental effects \cite{Trabzon2025Micromagnetic}. In contrast, the inverse problem aims to infer the underlying physical parameters responsible for a given observed domain configuration, such as those obtained from MFM or SP-STM \cite{Sass2020MagneticImaging}. This task is intrinsically ill-posed: multiple parameter combinations can reproduce visually similar textures, leading to degeneracy and uncertainty in parameter estimation \cite{Feng2024}.

Bridging these two directions—ensuring coherence between generation (direct mapping) and estimation (inverse mapping)—is essential for predictive materials discovery \cite{Feng2024}. A unified and physically consistent framework that can simultaneously learn both directions offers a pathway toward explainable, data-driven modeling of nanoscale magnetic phenomena \cite{Kovacs2023PhysicsInformed}.

Overall, the study of magnetic nanodots is constrained by three fundamental challenges that motivate the development of hybrid approaches combining physical modeling and data-driven inference:

\vspace{0.5cm}

\subsection{Degeneracy and Non-Uniqueness of Magnetic States}


\vspace{0.5cm}

Theoretical and computational approaches based on Hamiltonian formulations have become the dominant framework for studying magnetic nanodots due to their ability to provide physically grounded predictions under controlled parameter conditions. Micromagnetic simulations and atomistic spin dynamics solve the LLG equation or minimize the magnetic Hamiltonian to predict equilibrium and dynamic states \cite{Li2023Magnetic}. This approach offers several critical advantages: it enables exploration of parameter regimes inaccessible to experiment, provides complete three-dimensional vector field information at arbitrary resolution, and allows systematic investigation of competing magnetic interactions—exchange, anisotropy, DMI, Zeeman coupling—without measurement-induced perturbations \cite{Misiurev2024Modeling}. Moreover, simulations can incorporate thermal fluctuations, boundary conditions, and time-dependent external fields with explicit physical control, making them indispensable for understanding emergent phenomena such as skyrmion nucleation, domain wall dynamics, and magnetization reversal pathways \cite{Reichhardt2022Skyrmions}.

However, despite their physical rigor, Hamiltonian-based simulations expose a first fundamental source of non-uniqueness: the energetic degeneracy of magnetic states. Distinct combinations of parameters—exchange, anisotropy, and DMI—can relax into energetically comparable and visually similar magnetic patterns \cite{Feng2024}. Figure~\ref{fig:degeneracy_problem} illustrates this empirically: representative spin textures laid out over pairs of Hamiltonian parameters—temperature against a competing (frustrated) exchange interaction and against the DMI proxy $K_{DM}$—exhibit striking visual similarity across distinct parameter regimes, creating fundamental ambiguity for inverse estimation \cite{Park2024}.

Physically, this degeneracy originates in the structure of the magnetic energy landscape. As sketched in Figure~\ref{fig:energy_landscape}, the Hamiltonian admits multiple local minima corresponding to metastable spin textures—vortices, skyrmions, stripe or labyrinthine domains—that can be stabilized under different parameter sets \cite{Zhou2020Topological,Reichhardt2022Skyrmions}. Small compensations between competing interactions preserve the overall energy balance: an increase in anisotropy may be offset by stronger exchange or weaker DMI, yielding morphologically indistinguishable configurations \cite{Solovyev2021Exchange,Swindells2020Proximity}. Thermal fluctuations and boundary effects further blur these distinctions by enabling transitions between nearby minima \cite{Verma2020Picosecond}. As a result, the mapping from physical parameters to domain patterns is non-injective, introducing intrinsic ambiguity into the inverse identification of Hamiltonian parameters \cite{Besnard2025Magnetic}.

It is worth making the notion of degeneracy physically precise, since the term is often used loosely to mean mere visual similarity. In its strict sense, two configurations are degenerate when they share the same energy, $\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{S})=\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{S}')$, and such exact degeneracies are generically the signature of a symmetry of the Hamiltonian: whenever a transformation $g$ leaves $\mathcal{H}$ invariant, $\mathbf{S}$ and $g\mathbf{S}$ are strictly degenerate. Within this framework the ambiguity relevant to inverse estimation separates into three physically distinct regimes. \textbf{(i)}~\emph{Symmetry-induced (exact) degeneracy}, in which configurations related by a symmetry operation—global spin rotation for isotropic exchange, lattice translation of a domain pattern, or the mirror/parity operation that exchanges the two chiralities when the antisymmetric coupling vanishes—are rigorously iso-energetic. \textbf{(ii)}~\emph{Frustration-induced degeneracy}, in which competing interactions that cannot be simultaneously minimized (as in the exchange-frustrated regime governed by the second-neighbour coupling) flatten the energy landscape into a manifold of nearly iso-energetic textures. \textbf{(iii)}~\emph{Accidental (quasi-)degeneracy}, in which distinct parameter sets happen to yield comparable energies without any underlying symmetry, through compensations such as increased anisotropy offset by stronger exchange. The visual indistinguishability emphasized above is the observable fingerprint of these three mechanisms rather than their definition, and recognizing which one dominates a given region of parameter space is what makes the ambiguity physically interpretable rather than a generic nuisance. The inverse problem is non-unique for a second, distinct reason that arises not from the energy landscape but from the observable itself. Because the accessible measurement resolves only the out-of-plane component $s_z$, it is blind to the in-plane handedness of the texture: reversing the sign of the Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya interaction mirrors the in-plane spins and inverts the chirality while leaving the observed $s_z$ pattern unchanged \cite{MimicaFigari2020DMI}. Consequently, configurations of opposite chirality—though physically distinct and both experimentally realizable—map to identical $s_z$ observations, so the sign of the antisymmetric coupling is intrinsically unrecoverable from a single projection. This chiral degeneracy is a property of the measurement rather than of the simulation, and it becomes an exact energetic degeneracy in the achiral limit ($K_{DM}\to 0$), where both chiralities are equally probable.

\subsection{Sim-to-Real Gap}\label{sec:sim2real}

The study of magnetic domain structures is fundamentally constrained by a persistent discrepancy between computational predictions and experimental observations—a phenomenon known as the sim-to-real gap \cite{Wagenmaker2024SimToReal}. Computational modeling, whether based on atomistic spin dynamics or continuum micromagnetics, provides idealized representations of magnetic configurations derived from well-defined Hamiltonians and controlled numerical environments \cite{MacielDeMenezes2021Skyrmionics}. In contrast, experimental characterization techniques capture the physical reality of magnetic textures, but do so through indirect, technique-dependent measurements that are inevitably affected by instrumental artifacts, environmental perturbations, and the intrinsic limitations of each imaging modality \cite{Nistico2020Magnetic}. This fundamental asymmetry introduces systematic biases that manifest as domain-specific characteristics in both simulated and experimental data: computational approaches tend to produce smooth, noise-free, and boundary-condition-constrained spin fields, while experimental images exhibit instrumental noise, resolution-dependent blurring, contrast distortions, and measurement-induced perturbations \cite{Feng2024}. The resulting discrepancy poses a critical challenge for data-driven frameworks trained predominantly on synthetic datasets but intended for deployment in experimental workflows, as learned representations may fail to generalize across the simulation-experiment divide \cite{Hilgers2025}.

On the experimental side, magnetic domain images are inherently indirect representations of the underlying magnetization field. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:experimental_domain_shift}, techniques such as MFM measure the stray magnetic field above the sample surface through tip-sample interactions, introducing convolution effects, tip-induced artifacts, and resolution-dependent blurring (typically 30–50 nm) that obscure the true spin structure \cite{Jaber2023Magnetic}. SP-STM, despite offering atomic-scale resolution (~1 nm), is sensitive to electronic density of states and tunnel current asymmetries, which modulate the observed magnetic contrast in ways that depend on tip magnetization, bias voltage, and surface electronic structure; however, it requires ultra-high vacuum (UHV) and cryogenic conditions, making it operationally expensive (800–1500 USD/hour) and inaccessible for routine characterization \cite{Odobesko2024STM}. Lorentz transmission electron microscopy (5–10 nm resolution) captures projected magnetic induction, suffering from defocus-dependent phase shifts, integration effects along the beam path, and complex sample preparation requirements \cite{Plitzko2024Lorentz}. As summarized in Table~\ref{tab:experimental_costs_monetary}, each technique presents a distinct trade-off between spatial resolution, quantitative accuracy, operational complexity, and cost. Moreover, environmental factors—substrate roughness, thermal drift, electromagnetic interference—introduce pixel-level noise and spatial distortions that vary across measurement sessions and experimental setups \cite{Mukhatov2023Comprehensive}. Collectively, these effects mean that experimental datasets are not direct snapshots of magnetic configurations, but rather domain-specific transformations shaped by the physical principles and technical limitations of each imaging modality.

Beyond noise and finite resolution, the deepest component of the gap is not statistical but structural: an \emph{observable} gap between what the simulation knows and what the instrument records. A simulation resolves the full three-dimensional spin field $\mathbf{s}_i=(s_{x},s_{y},s_{z})$ at every site, whereas an accessible measurement returns a single scalar projection, so the comparison is mediated by an observation operator that discards information irreversibly. Crucially, this operator is technique-dependent and does not return the raw magnetization: magnetic force microscopy senses the out-of-plane derivative of the stray field above the surface rather than $\mathbf{s}$ itself; polar Kerr and normal-incidence STXM report essentially the out-of-plane component $s_{z}$; Lorentz transmission electron microscopy integrates the in-plane magnetic induction along the beam; and dichroic techniques project the magnetization onto the beam direction. There is therefore no single ``experimental observable'' but a family of projection operators, none of which coincides with the vector field produced by the simulation. Each projection is moreover convolved with the instrument transfer function (tip or point-spread response), a deterministic transformation absent from the idealized simulation even in the complete absence of noise. Consequently, closing the sim-to-real gap cannot be reduced to injecting noise into synthetic images: it requires modelling the observation operator itself—a physics-based forward model of the measurement—so that simulated and experimental data are compared in the same observable space.

On the computational side, numerical simulations introduce their own domain-specific characteristics. Atomistic spin dynamics operate on discrete lattices with fixed exchange interactions and often employ simplified anisotropy models or neglect long-range dipolar fields for computational tractability \cite{Holt2025Virtual}. Micromagnetic solvers discretize the continuum magnetization field into finite elements or finite differences, smoothing out sub-mesh-scale features and introducing numerical diffusion through time-stepping schemes in LLG integration \cite{Pfeiler2022Numerical}. Boundary conditions—periodic, open, or fixed-spin—impose artificial constraints that can suppress edge effects or nucleation phenomena relevant to finite-sized nanodots. Thermal fluctuations are either omitted entirely or approximated via stochastic fields whose amplitude and correlation properties depend on the chosen numerical protocol \cite{Sudsom2021Micromagnetic}. Consequently, even among simulated datasets, variations in discretization, solver algorithms, and physical approximations create distinct "computational domains" with systematically different textural features, contrast, and statistical properties compared to experimental counterparts \cite{Feng2024}. This simulation-induced domain bias is further amplified when models trained on one computational framework are applied to data generated by another, or when synthetic training data fail to capture the stochastic, defect-driven, and measurement-limited nature of real experimental observations \cite{Hilgers2025}.

\subsection{Lack of physic-based interpretability}
A persistent challenge in data-driven modeling of magnetic systems is the lack of in-
terpretability and physical traceability in the results. While modern machine learning
methods can efficiently approximate the complex mapping between Hamiltonian pa-
rameters and magnetic domain configurations, their internal representations are often
opaque, providing limited insight into the underlying physics that govern the observed
phenomena \cite{Park2024}. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:interpretability_challenge}, this opacity complicates the validation of learned relationships against es-
tablished physical laws, such as exchange symmetry, anisotropy effects, or DMI-induced
chirality \cite{Ahmadi2024PhysicsInformed}. Furthermore, when combined with noisy experimental data or degenerate
parameter spaces, black-box models may produce accurate reconstructions without
preserving causal or physically meaningful dependencies \cite{Liao2023}. Consequently, the absence
of interpretability not only limits the scientific reliability of such approaches but also
hinders their use in guiding material design and hypothesis generation \cite{Sahin2025Unlocking}.

A further, more subtle difficulty concerns \emph{how} interpretability is obtained. As sketched in Figure~\ref{fig:interpretability_challenge}, the prevailing strategy is \emph{post hoc}: an opaque model is trained first and then probed externally by attribution methods (Grad-CAM, regression activation maps, or Shapley-value analysis) to estimate which input regions drove a prediction. Such explanations are valuable but only \emph{describe} a fixed model; they cannot \emph{guarantee} that its internal reasoning respects the governing physics, and in degenerate or noisy regimes they may even attribute importance to spurious correlations that the model exploited to fit the data. An alternative is to make interpretability \emph{intrinsic}, embedding physical structure—symmetries, conservation constraints, or physically meaningful observables—directly into the architecture so that consistency with the underlying physics is enforced by construction rather than inspected after the fact. Which of these routes is appropriate, and to what extent physically hard-wired design can complement or replace post-hoc analysis for Hamiltonian parameter estimation, is itself an open question that this work must confront; a detailed comparison of the available techniques is deferred to Section~\ref{sec:state_art_interp}.

The three outlined challenges---degeneracy and non-uniqueness, the sim-to-real gap, and the lack of physics-based interpretability---are deeply interconnected within the study of magnetic domain systems \cite{Feng2024}. Degeneracy reflects the intrinsic non-uniqueness of the mapping between Hamiltonian parameters and magnetic configurations, arising both from energetic mechanisms (symmetry, frustration, and accidental compensation) and from the partial observability of single-axis measurements, which conflates configurations of opposite chirality \cite{Meissner2021Potts}. The sim-to-real gap, in turn, separates idealized synthetic data from scarce and costly experimental images---not only through noise, instrumental artifacts, and discretization, but more fundamentally through the observation operator that projects the full spin field onto the single scalar quantity each technique records \cite{Misiurev2024Modeling}. Finally, the lack of interpretability in data-driven models prevents the extraction of causal, physically meaningful relationships from these imperfect and ambiguous datasets, and raises the further question of whether physical consistency should be enforced by construction or only inspected post hoc \cite{Park2024}. Together, these issues define a landscape where conventional simulation-based approaches struggle with scalability and uncertainty, while purely data-driven models risk overfitting and opacity \cite{Luo2020Current}. This convergence of physical ambiguity, observational limitation, and epistemic opacity motivates the development of hybrid, physics-informed frameworks capable of enforcing physical consistency while enabling explainable inference across the direct and inverse magnetic domain problems \cite{Wu2024PhysicsInformed}.

\subsection{Fragmentation and Non-Reusability of Domain Knowledge}

The three challenges above are typically addressed in isolation, and even when solved they yield fragmented artifacts: a simulation engine, an inverse estimator, a generative model, and a set of interpretability tools, each living in a separate codebase, trained under its own conventions, and documented across heterogeneous scripts and publications. This fragmentation is itself a barrier. The physical knowledge encoded in these modules---how a texture responds to a parameter, which regions drive a prediction, how a configuration transitions under temperature or field---remains locked in disconnected, expert-operated pipelines, with no unified, queryable representation through which the modules can be composed, reused, or reasoned over as a coherent whole. This mirrors a broader concern in materials science, where fragmented data systems, non-standardized formats, and the absence of shared metadata and ontologies are recognized as central obstacles to interoperability and reproducibility \cite{brinson2023community,ghiringhelli2023shared}.

As a consequence, reproducing a result or chaining these capabilities (e.g., simulate $\rightarrow$ estimate $\rightarrow$ interpret $\rightarrow$ regenerate) requires deep, tacit expertise and manual glue code, which limits accessibility for non-specialists and prevents autonomous systems from exploiting the workflow. In adjacent fields, standardized, protocol-based interfaces have begun to expose analytical tools as composable capabilities that external orchestrators---including AI agents---can invoke in a controlled, auditable way \cite{hou2025mcp,gridach2025agentic}, and physics-aware multi-agent systems have already been demonstrated for materials design tasks such as alloy discovery \cite{ghafarollahi2024atomagents}; no such substrate exists for nanoscale magnetic-domain analysis, where the tooling is bespoke and physics-specific. The absence of an interoperable knowledge layer therefore stands as a distinct, integrative challenge: not a lack of individual models, but the lack of a coherent, reusable representation that unifies them.

Addressing it turns the collection of methods developed for the previous objectives from a loose set of tools into a single, coherent, and reusable system, and is the natural capstone that binds the direct problem, the inverse problem, and their interpretation into one physically grounded workflow.

\section{State of the art}

\subsection{Classical and Physics-Informed AI Approaches for Degeneracy Mitigation}

The state of the art has therefore evolved along two complementary lines of work: efforts to make the forward mapping more discriminative and physically grounded, and efforts to make the inverse mapping more expressive, uncertainty-aware, and physically constrained. What follows is a synthesis of the major developments, their strengths and limitations, and the structural gaps that motivate new approaches.

\paragraph{High-Fidelity Forward Simulations.}
Research on the direct problem has traditionally relied on high-fidelity micromagnetic or atomistic simulations capable of capturing the subtle competition between exchange, anisotropy, DMI, dipolar interactions, and applied fields. Tools such as MuMax3, OOMMF, and Spirit have enabled precise modeling of domain walls, skyrmions, vortices, and labyrinthine patterns under a wide range of physical conditions \cite{Li2023Magnetic}. These approaches are widely used in both micrometer-scale and nanoscale magnetism, particularly in the study of skyrmions, chiral domain walls, and confined spin textures in ultrathin films. When coupled with forward-rendering models that explicitly account for instrument response—such as the point-spread function of MFM, the tunneling convolution kernel in SP-STM, or noise induced by probe electronics—these simulators generate synthetic images that closely resemble their experimental counterparts \cite{Li2023Magnetic}. This integration has been essential in applications such as skyrmion detection, micromagnetic parameter inference, and interpretation of high-resolution imaging data.

A complementary approach to continuum micromagnetics is the use of classical Monte Carlo methods combined with Heisenberg-type Hamiltonians, particularly effective for modeling nanogranular magnetic structures. Agudelo Giraldo \cite{AgudeloGiraldo2018} developed a comprehensive framework for simulating nanogranular magnetic thin films, where virtual grains were generated following lognormal volume distributions consistent with experimental observations. The structural relaxation at grain boundaries was modeled using the Morse potential under Monte Carlo relaxation, while magnetic interactions were simulated via the classical Heisenberg model with cubic magnetocrystalline anisotropy. The Hamiltonian incorporated four key contributions: (i) nearest-neighbor exchange interaction with distance-dependent coupling, (ii) magnetocrystalline, surface, and grain-boundary anisotropy, (iii) external magnetic field influence, and (iv) dipolar interactions computed via a hybrid spin-spin and spin-cell approach with a cutoff radius. Disorder at grain boundaries was introduced through anisotropy vectors dependent on lattice deformations, and temperature-dependent anisotropy effects were explicitly accounted for. Magnetic state relaxation was performed using the Metropolis algorithm, and the code was parallelized to handle the computational cost, with samples divided into cells with shared memory access. This methodology successfully reproduced monodomain-to-multidomain transitions as a function of grain volume, film thickness, and boundary anisotropy parameters, revealing superparamagnetic effects and blocking temperatures in scenarios with strong interfacial anisotropy, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:monte_carlo_flow}. Such physically accurate models substantially reduce non-physical degeneracies arising from oversimplified imaging assumptions.

Nevertheless, these simulator-based approaches—whether continuum or atomistic—are computationally expensive and sensitive to numerical discretization, initialization conditions, and solver choices, all of which can artificially inflate the observed degeneracy. More importantly, even perfect numerical solvers cannot eliminate intrinsic degeneracy: distinct combinations of DMI, anisotropy, exchange, and dipolar strengths may legitimately yield nearly indistinguishable patterns. Thus, while forward simulations reveal the structure of degeneracy, they cannot resolve it.

\paragraph{Differentiable and Surrogate Models.}
To mitigate these limitations, recent work has turned to differentiable and surrogate forward models. Neural surrogates, variational approximators, and differentiable solvers offer orders-of-magnitude speedups and enable gradient-based calibration and end-to-end integration into machine-learning frameworks; it should be noted, however, that these differentiable-surrogate methodologies have been consolidated largely in \emph{other} physics and engineering domains---for example, surrogate optimization of large experimental facilities \cite{GallardoRomero2025Differentiable}---rather than for nanoscale magnetic-domain modeling, where their adoption is still incipient. A typical neural surrogate architecture (upper branch of Figure~\ref{fig:surrogate_pinn}) processes input physical parameters through dense layers to create feature embeddings, which are then upsampled via transposed convolutions to generate predicted spin configurations. These methods have been applied in problems such as rapid skyrmion-profile generation, uncertainty-aware forward prediction, and coarse-to-fine parameter scanning. While these models enable exploration of high-dimensional parameter spaces that are intractable for brute-force simulations, their accuracy is fundamentally limited by the diversity and physical realism of the training set. As a result, surrogate models may hallucinate non-physical spin textures or fail to generalize outside the training manifold. Consequently, this research line—although actively growing—is not yet considered a mature or commonly adopted tool for nanoscale domain reconstruction, highlighting the field's lack of physics-faithful, constraint-preserving differentiable solvers.

\paragraph{Physics-Informed Neural Networks.}
In parallel, physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) and hybrid modeling frameworks have gained traction by embedding the Landau–Lifshitz–Gilbert (LLG) equation or energetic constraints directly into the learning objective \cite{Karniadakis2021PINNs}. As shown in the lower branch of Figure~\ref{fig:surrogate_pinn}, PINNs augment the standard neural surrogate architecture with physics-based loss terms that enforce the LLG equation at collocation points throughout the spatiotemporal domain. It is essential to note that this family of methods operates in a fundamentally different regime from the task addressed here: the LLG residual describes the \emph{continuum, time-dependent} micromagnetic dynamics of the magnetization field, so LLG-based PINNs have been tested on complementary problems at other scales---reconstructing domain-wall dynamics, modeling low-dimensional continuum textures, or enforcing plausibility in surrogate outputs---and \emph{not} on the estimation of the parameters of an atomistic, extended-Heisenberg Hamiltonian from static thermally-relaxed images, which is the problem of this work. By injecting physical laws into the learning process they help restrict the feasible solution set, but PINNs for magnetic systems remain difficult to train due to the stiffness of the LLG equation, the balancing of data-fidelity and physics terms, and poor scalability to realistic thin-film geometries and finite-temperature regimes. Their use even within continuum magnetic modeling remains exploratory rather than widely adopted, and no PINN formulation has been reported for the atomistic Hamiltonian-inference task considered here.

The objective in using Deep Learning for the Forward Mapping (Surrogate Model) in micromagnetics is to construct a function that rapidly maps a low-dimensional input vector of Hamiltonian parameters to the resulting high-dimensional spin configuration image, bypassing slow micromagnetic simulations. Given a vector of $P$ physical parameters $\mathbf{y}_n \in \mathbb{R}^{P}$ (e.g., $D$, $K$, $J$, $H_{\text{ext}}$), the task is to predict the image $\mathbf{\hat{I}}_n \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times \tilde{W} \times C}$. The core of the standard Deep Learning approach is to construct a deterministic function $\mathbf{\hat{I}}_n = f_{\Theta}(\mathbf{y}_n)$, where $f_{\Theta} : \mathbb{R}^{P} \to \mathbb{R}^{H \times \tilde{W} \times C}$ is parameterized by a Deep Neural Network (DNN), such as a Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) or an up-sampling network (like a transposed CNN or U-Net variant). The architecture uses dense layers to process the input vector $\mathbf{y}_n$ into an initial feature map, followed by transposed convolutions ($\otimes^T$) to increase the spatial resolution:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:transposed_conv_feature}
\mathbf{F}_l = \sigma_l(\mathbf{W}_l \otimes^T \mathbf{F}_{l-1} + \mathbf{b}_l)
\end{equation}

The final layers project this embedding into the target image space, $\mathbf{\hat{I}}_n$. The trainable parameter set $\Theta$ is optimized by minimizing the expected loss between the ground-truth spin configuration $\mathbf{I}_n$ (from high-fidelity simulations) and the prediction $\mathbf{\hat{I}}_n$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:theta_optimization}
\Theta^{*} = \arg \min_{\Theta} \mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{I}_n, \mathbf{\hat{I}}_n | \Theta) : \forall n \in \mathbb{N}]
\end{equation}
where the common regression loss function $\mathcal{L}$ is the Mean Squared Error (MSE):
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mse_loss_surrogate}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}} = \frac{1}{N_{\text{data}} H \tilde{W} C} \sum_{n=1}^{N_{\text{data}}} || \mathbf{I}_n - \mathbf{\hat{I}}_n ||^2
\end{equation}
The critical flaw of this purely data-driven approach is its lack of physical guarantees and its inability to capture the inherent multimodality of magnetic states. Since it aims for a single deterministic output $\mathbf{\hat{I}}_n$ for a given input $\mathbf{y}_n$, it struggles to maintain physical consistency and may yield non-physical textures outside the training distribution, a fundamental problem in tackling degeneracy.

Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) resolve this by merging the data-driven loss with physical constraints. The key is to enforce that the predicted magnetization field $\mathbf{M}_{\text{NN}}$ (which corresponds to $\mathbf{\hat{I}}_n$) must satisfy the Landau--Lifshitz--Gilbert (LLG) equation, which governs the dynamics. The LLG equation is incorporated through its residual $\mathbf{R}_{\text{LLG}}(\mathbf{x}, t)$, where the necessary spatial and temporal derivatives are calculated using automatic differentiation on the NN's output:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:llg_residual}
\mathbf{R}_{\text{LLG}}(\mathbf{x}, t) = \frac{\partial \mathbf{M}}{\partial t} + \frac{\gamma}{1+\alpha^2} (\mathbf{M} \times \mathbf{H}_{\text{eff}}) + \frac{\gamma \alpha}{1+\alpha^2} \left( \mathbf{M} \times (\mathbf{M} \times \mathbf{H}_{\text{eff}}) \right) = \mathbf{0}
\end{equation}
The PINN modifies the optimization objective by adding a Physics Loss ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{physics}}$) and a Magnitude Constraint Loss ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{magnitude}}$) to the total function:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pinn_total_loss}
\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{data}} + \lambda_{\text{physics}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{physics}} + \lambda_{\text{magnitude}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{magnitude}}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{physics}}$ penalizes deviations from the LLG equation across collocation points $\mathcal{P}$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:physics_loss_pinn}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{physics}} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|} \sum_{(\mathbf{x}_i, t_i) \in \mathcal{P}} || \mathbf{R}_{\text{LLG}}(\mathbf{x}_i, t_i) ||^2
\end{equation}
And $\mathcal{L}_{\text{magnitude}}$ enforces that the magnitude remains constant ($||\mathbf{M}|| = M_s$):
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:magnitude_loss_pinn}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{magnitude}} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|} \sum_{(\mathbf{x}_i, t_i) \in \mathcal{P}} \left( || \mathbf{M}_{\text{NN}}(\mathbf{x}_i, t_i) ||^2 - M_s^2 \right)^2
\end{equation}
By minimizing this physics-informed loss, the network is constrained to produce outputs that are not only statistically accurate but also physically plausible, thereby reducing the non-physical solution space and offering a more robust approach to mitigating degeneracy.




A qualitatively different perspective has emerged with the use of generative models---variational autoencoders (VAEs), generative adversarial networks (GANs), and more recently diffusion models. Rather than a deterministic function, these models learn a probability distribution over configurations, enabling the explicit representation of multimodality. In their established form they are applied \emph{unconditionally}, sampling plausible structures from a learned prior, and have proven successful mostly in \emph{other} materials domains---for instance, diffusion-based discovery of atomic surface structures \cite{Ronne2024}---rather than for magnetic textures. Among these, denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPMs) have recently become the most competitive family: a fixed forward process gradually corrupts a configuration with Gaussian noise, and a U-Net learns to reverse it, optionally conditioned on external variables. Figure~\ref{fig:ddpm_baseline} illustrates such a conditional DDPM, following the architecture of Lee \emph{et al.}~\cite{lee2025thermodynamic}, which is adopted as the generative backbone replicated and extended in this work. The step of \emph{conditioning} such generators on physical parameters, which is central to the present work, has so far barely been explored for spin systems. Their ability to sample multiple plausible outputs for a given parameter set directly reflects the inherent degeneracy of magnetic states. Nevertheless, the existing efforts that apply conditional generative models to spin systems remain confined to substantially simpler settings than the one addressed in this work. Tellingly, the most recent representative studies are tied to \emph{simplified, low-dimensional spin models}: they generate states from datasets produced by the discrete $\mathbb{Z}_2$ Ising model or the planar (XY) model---systems with a single scalar coupling and a binary or single-angle degree of freedom---rather than the continuous, three-dimensional, multi-parameter extended Heisenberg Hamiltonian considered here \cite{lee2025thermodynamic, jha2026topological}. These reduced settings sidestep the very difficulties this work targets: they involve neither the eight-dimensional parameter space, nor the vector spin texture, nor the antisymmetric (DMI) chirality that drive degeneracy in realistic magnetic nanodots. In these works the generator is typically conditioned on a single scalar, most often the temperature, so that no parameter degeneracy is actually at play: with temperature as the only control variable, the conditioning is effectively one-to-one and the multimodality intrinsic to a multi-parameter Hamiltonian never arises. Moreover, these studies address only the forward generation step in isolation and do not close the full forward--inverse cycle, leaving the question of parameter identifiability unaddressed. By contrast, the present work conditions the generators on the complete eight-dimensional parameter vector $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ of an extended Heisenberg Hamiltonian and embeds them within a closed cycle that explicitly confronts parameter degeneracy. More broadly, generative models lack physical guarantees unless combined with physics-based constraints or energy-informed discriminators, and their outputs may deviate significantly from micromagnetic ground truth. To date, they remain promising but immature tools within nanoscale magnetism, with substantial gaps in validation, benchmarking, and physical consistency.

regression problem, typically using convolutional or fully connected neural networks
trained on large synthetic datasets \cite{Feng2024}. The closest in-field precedent is the
work of Feng \emph{et al.} \cite{Feng2024}, which classifies skyrmionic textures and regresses
Hamiltonian parameters from a two-dimensional Heisenberg model; however, it estimates only
three couplings (exchange $J$, DMI $D$, and anisotropy $K$) from micromagnetic textures,
treats classification and regression as separate supervised tasks, and neither closes a
forward--inverse cycle nor addresses the single-axis ($s_z$) observability and chirality
degeneracy central to this proposal. It thus illustrates the feasibility of the inverse
mapping while leaving open precisely the harder, higher-dimensional, and identifiability-aware
problem addressed here. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:supervised_regression}, these models are
computationally efficient and capable of predicting parameters in real time, making
them attractive for high-throughput applications in micromagnetism, spintronics, and
magnetic materials characterization. However, strict regression collapses the inherent
ambiguity of the inverse problem into a single point estimate, making predictions highly
sensitive to sim-to-exp mismatch and dataset bias. When two or more parameter regimes
produce nearly indistinguishable images, these models tend to yield unstable or
misleading predictions. Their dependence on large representative datasets introduces a
significant bottleneck, as the quality and diversity of synthetic training data are limited
by the accuracy, coverage, and numerical stability of the forward simulator.

The limitations of deterministic regression have motivated a transition toward Bayesian
inference methodologies, which represent the inverse mapping as a posterior
distribution instead of a single estimate. Formally, a prior $P(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ and a likelihood $P(\mathcal{D}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\theta})$ combine into a posterior $P(\boldsymbol{\theta}\,|\,\mathcal{D})$ whose multiple modes---a dominant peak at the maximum-a-posteriori estimate alongside secondary peaks at alternative, equally plausible parameter sets---directly expose the degeneracy as a multimodal, high-variance distribution. By explicitly modeling uncertainty and
multi-modality, Bayesian methods \emph{expose} the underlying degeneracy rather than hiding
it, offering richer insights into confidence levels, plausible parameter regions, and
non-identifiability---but it must be stressed that quantifying a multimodal posterior
characterizes the ambiguity without \emph{resolving} it: the estimator reports that several
parameter sets are compatible with the observation, yet still cannot single out the true one.
Such surrogate-accelerated Bayesian inversion is well established in adjacent inverse problems---for
instance, geo-electromagnetic data inversion \cite{Yang2024Bayesian}---but has seen only limited
application to nanoscale magnetic-domain Hamiltonian estimation.
Moreover, Bayesian inference remains computationally expensive because it
requires repeated forward evaluations—each often involving a full micromagnetic
simulation or a surrogate model. Posterior accuracy also depends critically on the form
of the likelihood and prior, which are difficult to specify for systems governed by the
Landau–Lifshitz–Gilbert equation and subject to complex magnetic interactions.

To address these challenges, cycle-consistent frameworks have recently emerged as a
promising direction. These approaches couple a forward surrogate with an inverse
estimator, enforcing that both are consistent with one another through a cyclic
constraint. By requiring that inferred parameters can regenerate the original image via
the forward model, these methods naturally filter out many non-physical or inconsistent
solutions. It is important to stress, however, that this idea has so far been developed
\emph{outside} the field of nanoscale magnetism: the cyclic forward--inverse formulation
originates in general data-driven PDE solution and parameter estimation, where it was
introduced for conditional generative adversarial networks
\cite{Kadeethum2021Framework}, an application domain entirely different from
micromagnetics. Within nanoscale magnetism itself, no closed forward--inverse cycle has
yet been reported; the literature provides only \emph{individual} models addressing each
subproblem separately --- an inverse estimator for parameter regression \emph{or} a
forward generator for state synthesis --- and typically built on simplified energy models
(e.g., Ising or single-axis XY Hamiltonians) rather than a full extended Heisenberg
description. Beyond this gap, cycle-consistent schemes also inherit all biases from the
forward surrogate; if the surrogate is inaccurate or poorly calibrated, the cycle
constraint may amplify---rather than mitigate---systematic errors. Their stability when
applied to micromagnetic data thus remains largely unexplored, and training can become
unstable in strongly degenerate regimes.

Hybrid inverse methods represent a more recent development, combining statistical
inference with physics-informed constraints. These include Bayesian Physics-Informed
Neural Networks (PINNs), energy-regularized inverse networks, and approaches that
penalize violations of the LLG equation or magnetic free-energy minimization. By embedding physical priors directly in
the inference process, these methods restrict predictions to physically interpretable and
energetically plausible regions of parameter space. Here too, however, the demonstrated
applications lie in \emph{adjacent} problems---macroscopic magnetic-target and geophysical
inversion \cite{Lu2024CGLS}, or continuum magnetization-dynamics inversion governed by the
LLG equation---rather than in the estimation of an atomistic extended-Heisenberg Hamiltonian
from thermally-relaxed $s_z$ images; the LLG-based variants, in particular, again presuppose
the continuum dynamical regime that does not describe the present task. Their main drawbacks lie in computational cost,
training stiffness, and the difficulty of sampling complex posteriors that remain highly
multimodal in degenerate regimes. Table~\ref{tab:sota_inverse_magnetism} provides a comprehensive comparison of these inverse modeling approaches.

\subsection{Domain Adaptation Strategies for Sim-to-Real Transfer}

As described in the problem statement (Section~\ref{sec:sim2real}), the sim-to-real gap represents a fundamental challenge in applying data-driven models trained on simulated magnetic domain data to experimental observations \cite{Hilgers2025}. The systematic distribution shifts between computational predictions and experimental measurements—arising from idealized physics in simulations versus instrumental artifacts, noise, and measurement-specific transformations in experiments—cause learned models to fail when deployed in real-world scenarios \cite{Feng2024}. This section reviews the state of the art in domain adaptation techniques that have been developed to bridge the simulation-experiment divide, with particular emphasis on methods applicable to magnetic domain imaging and related materials characterization problems. The literature on domain adaptation has matured significantly over the past decade, encompassing data-level transformations, feature-level alignment, model-level transfer, and hybrid approaches that combine multiple strategies \cite{Wilson2020Survey}. While originally developed for computer vision tasks (object recognition, semantic segmentation), these methods have shown promise in scientific imaging domains where synthetic training data is abundant but real experimental data is scarce and expensive \cite{Sim2RealReview2021}.

\paragraph{Domain adaptation: overview and categorization.}
Bridging the simulation–experiment gap is commonly addressed through domain adaptation and transfer learning. Domain adaptation techniques enable models trained on source domains (typically simulated data) to generalize effectively to target domains (experimental observations) despite systematic distribution shifts \cite{Wilson2020Survey}. Figure~\ref{fig:da_overview} illustrates the fundamental concept: transferring knowledge from a data-rich source domain to a data-scarce target domain despite their distribution gap. These methods can be broadly categorized into several complementary approaches, each addressing different aspects of the domain shift problem.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{Figures/domain_adaptation_overview.pdf}
    \caption{Domain adaptation overview: transferring knowledge from simulation (source domain) to experiment (target domain) despite systematic distribution shifts.}
    \label{fig:da_overview}
\end{figure}

\subparagraph{Data-Based Domain Adaptation.}
Data-based approaches modify the training data itself to match the target domain distribution through augmentation (rotation, scaling, noise injection), synthetic degradation mimicking experimental artifacts (blur, resolution reduction), and domain mixing strategies \cite{DeGersem2020Magnetic}. While computationally efficient and requiring no architectural changes, these methods depend on accurate modeling of the real data distribution and may require extensive manual tuning \cite{Chertenkov2025Machine}.

\subparagraph{Instance-Based Domain Adaptation.}
Instance-based methods adapt at the sample level by reweighting training instances according to their importance for the target domain or generating pseudo-labels for unlabeled target data through self-training \cite{Singhal2023Domain}. These approaches often incorporate test-time adaptation mechanisms that fine-tune model parameters on-the-fly using target domain statistics \cite{Wang2021TentTTA}. While flexible and requiring no target labels, they depend on reliable confidence calibration and can be sensitive to large distribution shifts \cite{Deng2022Dynamic}.

\subparagraph{Feature-Based Domain Adaptation.}
Feature-based methods align learned representations across domains by minimizing statistical discrepancies in intermediate feature spaces \cite{Farahani2021Brief}. Common strategies include Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) matching feature moments, CORAL aligning second-order statistics, and Domain-Adversarial Neural Networks (DANN) learning domain-invariant features through adversarial training \cite{Singhal2023Domain}. These approaches are theoretically grounded but require careful hyperparameter tuning and may inadvertently discard task-relevant discriminative features \cite{Zhang2020Unsupervised}.

\subparagraph{Adversarial Domain Adaptation.}
Adversarial approaches explicitly train domain discriminators alongside task predictors, leveraging gradient reversal layers to encourage feature extractors to produce domain-confounding representations \cite{Qin2020Multicenter}. The feature extractor maximizes domain confusion while the discriminator attempts to distinguish source from target samples, creating a minimax game driving domain-invariant representation learning \cite{Zhu2024Survey}. While powerful and end-to-end trainable, these methods can suffer from training instability and sensitivity to hyperparameters \cite{Yang2023Deep}.

\subparagraph{Autoencoder-Based Domain Adaptation.}
Autoencoder-based methods learn shared latent representations by training encoders and decoders to reconstruct both source and target domain inputs from a common bottleneck \cite{Dev2022Deep}. The reconstruction constraint encourages the latent space to capture domain-invariant structural information while discarding domain-specific noise \cite{Liu2021Domain}. Task predictors operate on these shared representations, enabling transfer without explicit domain labels. However, reconstruction objectives may preserve task-irrelevant information or lose discriminative features \cite{Rostami2022Transfer}.

\subparagraph{Model-Based Domain Adaptation.}
Model-based approaches leverage pre-trained models from source domains and adapt them to target domains through transfer learning, fine-tuning, or feature extraction \cite{Zhuang2020Comprehensive}. Common strategies include full fine-tuning of all network layers on limited target data, selective fine-tuning of task-specific layers while freezing feature extractors, and using pre-trained features as fixed representations with new task heads \cite{Kumar2022FineTuning}. These methods are particularly effective when domains share structural similarities and target data is scarce, but risk negative transfer if domains are too dissimilar \cite{Mp2024Mitigating}.

\subparagraph{Hybrid Domain Adaptation.}
Hybrid approaches combine multiple adaptation strategies to leverage their complementary strengths and mitigate individual limitations \cite{Dayal2025Leveraging}. A typical hybrid framework might integrate data augmentation for source domain enrichment, feature alignment through MMD or CORAL, adversarial training for domain confusion, transfer learning for initialization, and instance weighting for sample importance) \cite{Singhal2023Domain}. By operating at data, feature, and model levels simultaneously, hybrid methods achieve robust performance across diverse domain shifts, though at increased implementation complexity and computational cost \cite{Singhal2023Domain}.

\paragraph{Beyond statistical alignment: physics-aware and self-supervised alternatives.}
The strategies reviewed above---data, instance, feature, adversarial, autoencoder, model, and hybrid adaptation---share a common premise: they treat the domain gap as a \emph{statistical} discrepancy to be minimized in a black-box feature space (typically through MMD or CORAL moment matching), largely agnostic to the physics that generated the images. This is a limitation, because in magnetic-domain imaging the shift is not arbitrary but governed by the physics of the measurement (Section~\ref{sec:sim2real}). A complementary and increasingly prominent family of methods instead exploits domain knowledge, and four such alternatives are especially relevant here.

\subparagraph{Physics-Based Forward Modeling.} Rather than aligning distributions after the fact, this approach models the \emph{observation operator} itself---the physical image-formation process of the instrument (projection, point-spread function, noise statistics)---so that synthetic data are rendered into the same observable space as experiment. The imaging physics-based data-synthesis paradigm has been formalized for magnetic-resonance imaging, where signals are generated directly from the governing physical equations to train models with little or no real data \cite{yang2023physics}. This route is the natural counterpart to the observable gap identified in Section~\ref{sec:sim2real}: it attacks the discrepancy at its physical source rather than masking it statistically.

\subparagraph{Domain Randomization.} Here the parameters of the simulator (noise level, resolution, contrast, instrumental artifacts) are broadly randomized during training so that the real domain appears as merely one more sample of a wide synthetic distribution, enabling zero-shot transfer without any target-domain data. Recent theoretical analysis clarifies when and why this succeeds by modeling the simulator as a family of parameterized environments \cite{chen2022understanding}.

\subparagraph{Physics-Guided Image-to-Image Translation.} Generative translation models (e.g., CycleGAN variants) learn a mapping between the simulated and experimental image manifolds while constraints preserve physically meaningful content. In electron microscopy, a CycleGAN with a reciprocal-space discriminator was used to render simulated images nearly indistinguishable from experiment while retaining ground-truth labels for downstream learning \cite{khan2023leveraging}---a setting closely analogous to magnetic-domain microscopy.

\subparagraph{Contrastive Self-Supervised Learning.} Instead of explicit distribution matching, contrastive objectives learn representations that are invariant to domain-specific nuisances by pulling together augmented or cross-domain views of the same content and pushing apart different ones, yielding domain-robust features without target labels \cite{long2022domain}.

Crucially, none of these four physics-aware paradigms has yet been established for nanoscale magnetic-domain imaging: each has been demonstrated in an \emph{adjacent} field---robotics and reinforcement learning for domain randomization \cite{chen2022understanding}, natural-language processing for contrastive unsupervised adaptation \cite{long2022domain}, biomedical magnetic-resonance imaging for physics-based synthesis \cite{yang2023physics}, and electron microscopy for physics-guided translation \cite{khan2023leveraging}---but not for the estimation of Hamiltonian parameters from magnetic textures. The same caveat applies to the statistical alignment methods reviewed earlier, whose validation lies overwhelmingly in general computer vision rather than in magnetic imaging. This absence of a domain-specific, physically grounded sim-to-real methodology is precisely the gap this work targets.

The choice among all these strategies depends on the specific characteristics of the sim-to-real gap in magnetic domain imaging: the availability of target domain data, the nature and magnitude of distribution shifts, computational constraints, and the interpretability requirements of the application \cite{Wilson2020Survey}. For magnetic nanodot systems, where experimental data is expensive and limited while simulation data is abundant but systematically biased, the most rigorous path is unlikely to be purely statistical alignment; rather, physics-aware strategies---particularly physics-based forward modeling of the observation operator, optionally combined with domain randomization and self-supervised invariances---offer a principled route to robust sim-to-real generalization that respects the physical origin of the gap \cite{Hilgers2025,yang2023physics}.

\paragraph{Summary and research gaps.}
Despite significant progress in domain adaptation methods, several challenges remain specifically relevant to magnetic domain imaging. First, most domain adaptation techniques assume smooth, continuous distribution shifts, whereas the simulation-experiment gap in magnetic imaging involves discrete instrumental modalities (MFM vs. SP-STM vs. Lorentz TEM) with fundamentally different physical measurement principles \cite{Guan2021Domain}. Second, current methods often require access to unlabeled target domain data during training, which may not always be available for expensive experimental techniques \cite{Chronopoulou2024Efficient}. Third, domain adaptation approaches typically focus on classification or regression tasks, with limited exploration of generative modeling for magnetic textures where both forward and inverse problems must be addressed simultaneously \cite{Park2024}. Finally, the interaction between domain adaptation and physics-informed constraints remains underexplored: how to preserve physical consistency (energy minimization, micromagnetic equilibrium) while adapting across domains is an open research question \cite{Sun2022Applications}.


\subsection{Post-Hoc Attribution and Physics-Guided Interpretability Methods}\label{sec:state_art_interp}

A persistent challenge in data-driven modeling of magnetic systems is the lack of interpretability and physical traceability in learned models. This limitation is not unique to magnetism: across computational physics and materials science, neural surrogates often match or surpass classical models in accuracy yet remain opaque, making it difficult to validate learned relationships against established physical laws or to extract causal insight \cite{Oviedo2022}. In nanoscale magnetism this opacity is particularly problematic, as inferred material parameters (exchange, anisotropy, DMI, external field, temperature) must be numerically precise and physically meaningful to support device design, hypothesis testing, and experimental planning.

Recent work (2020–2025) addressing interpretability in physics-informed learning can be grouped into four main directions: (1) post-hoc attribution and saliency methods adapted to physics data, (2) architectures aligned with Hamiltonian structure or physical operators, (3) cycle-consistent and energy-aware hybrid models that enforce forward--inverse coherence, and (4) probabilistic and disentangled latent models that expose uncertainty and factorized physical degrees of freedom. Below we summarize advances, strengths, limitations, and open gaps relevant to micromagnetic modeling.

\paragraph{Post-hoc attribution and local explainers.}
Classical explainability methods---such as saliency maps, occlusion tests, Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM), Integrated Gradients, and perturbation analyses---have been adapted to materials microstructure problems and magnetization textures \cite{Oviedo2022}. These tools highlight image regions most strongly influencing model predictions, enabling researchers to verify that regressors attend to physically meaningful structures (domain walls, skyrmion cores, vortex centers) rather than spurious artefacts or background noise (\cref{fig:gradcam_attribution}). Their advantages are simplicity, model-agnostic applicability, and utility as diagnostic screening tools. However, they provide only descriptive explanations rather than causal or physically grounded insight, and attribution maps can be unstable to small perturbations or architectural changes \cite{Talapatra2022Understanding}.

The core limitation arises from the independence assumption inherent in both SHAP and LIME: these methods treat each input feature (in the case of images, each pixel) as an independently perturbable entity whose contribution can be isolated and quantified \cite{Kumar2020Problems}. This assumption is reasonable for tabular datasets with genuinely independent features (e.g., patient age, blood pressure, cholesterol level) but becomes physically meaningless when applied to spatially continuous fields governed by differential equations. In magnetic domain images, pixel values represent discretized samples of continuous magnetization vector fields $\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{r})$ subject to exchange coupling, which enforces smoothness and correlation between neighboring spins. Perturbing or masking individual pixels---as required by SHAP and LIME---violates micromagnetic continuity, breaks exchange energy minimization, and produces configurations that cannot exist as equilibrium magnetic states \cite{Li2025Explainable}. The resulting feature importance scores therefore reflect model sensitivities to non-physical perturbations rather than physically interpretable causal relationships (\cref{fig:shap_lime_limitations}).

Furthermore, SHAP's computational cost scales exponentially with the number of features, making exact computation intractable for high-resolution images (e.g., $128 \times 128$ pixels yield 16,384 features). Approximation methods such as Kernel SHAP rely on sampling strategies that may fail to capture long-range spatial correlations and collective magnetic structures (skyrmion lattices, stripe domains) whose physical significance emerges from coherent multi-pixel configurations rather than individual pixel contributions \cite{Covert2021Explaining}. LIME faces similar issues: its local linear surrogates are constructed by generating perturbed samples in a neighborhood around the prediction of interest, but defining meaningful neighborhoods for spatial field data is non-trivial, and random pixel perturbations do not respect the underlying physics \cite{Raptis2024Pixels}. For these reasons, while SHAP and LIME remain valuable diagnostic tools for identifying model behavior inconsistencies or dataset biases, they are insufficient for validating physical consistency or extracting domain-specific causal insight in continuous field inverse problems. Specialized methods that respect spatial structure (e.g., superpixel-based segmentation, physics-aware perturbations) or leverage domain knowledge (e.g., energy decompositions, latent space disentanglement) are required to achieve interpretability aligned with micromagnetic physics \cite{Achtibat2023Attribution}.

\paragraph{Architectures aligned with physics.}
A growing body of work embeds physical structure directly into model architectures. Examples include Hamiltonian- and energy-preserving networks, equivariant architectures for magnetic and electronic structure, operator-aware convolutions, and graph-based models for spin lattices \cite{Dornheim2023Neural}. Such models improve traceability by making internal components correspond to energy terms or symmetry representations; they enable per-term energy decomposition, sensitivity analysis, and physically meaningful extrapolation. Their main limitation is the need to predefine relevant physical terms and symmetries, which can reduce flexibility when unknown or emergent interactions are present.

\paragraph{Cycle-consistency, energy penalties, and hybrid forward--inverse training.}
Cycle-consistent frameworks pair a forward operator (parameters $\rightarrow$ texture) with an inverse model (texture $\rightarrow$ parameters) and enforce reconstruction and energy-based coherence \cite{Huang2023}. When the forward operator is a physics-informed simulator or a calibrated differentiable surrogate, cycle consistency acts as an interpretability constraint: predicted parameters must regenerate the input texture under the forward physics model. Additional energy penalties (differences in Hamiltonian energy between predicted and observed states) further restrict non-physical solutions. These methods have been used for uncertainty quantification and robust inversion in inverse-imaging and geophysical applications, and are beginning to be adapted to micromagnetic tasks. Their main weakness remains the fidelity of the forward module: surrogate bias can be propagated and reinforced.

\paragraph{Probabilistic models, uncertainty quantification, and disentangled latent spaces.}
Probabilistic generative models—normalizing flows, variational inference, and Bayesian neural networks—provide full posterior descriptions and explicit multimodality, making degeneracy and non-identifiability observable in the posterior landscape \cite{Wei2022}. Disentanglement methods (e.g., $\beta$-VAE variations) have been adapted to physical fields to align latent axes with generative factors, aiding interpretability and enabling targeted interventions in latent space \cite{Jacobsen2022}. Normalizing flows and flow-based variational methods have also been integrated with iterative solvers for inverse problems to produce calibrated posteriors and actionable uncertainty estimates \cite{Wei2022}. These probabilistic approaches are computationally demanding and require careful prior and likelihood modeling, but they offer the most principled route to exposing degeneracy. \subsection{Agentic AI and Interoperable Knowledge Substrates}\label{sec:state_art_agents}

The fourth challenge identified in Section~\ref{sec:problem_statement}---the fragmentation and non-reusability of domain knowledge---has no established solution in nanoscale magnetism, but the enabling technologies have matured rapidly in adjacent fields. This subsection reviews the three lines of work most relevant to a unified, agent-orientable knowledge substrate: autonomous scientific agents, tool-interoperability protocols, and knowledge representation for reusability.

\paragraph{Agentic AI and LLM agents for scientific discovery.}
Large language models have evolved from passive text generators into \emph{agents} that plan, reason, invoke external tools, and act autonomously within a scientific workflow \cite{gridach2025agentic}. In chemistry, Coscientist couples a language model with web search, documentation retrieval, code execution, and laboratory automation to design and run experiments end-to-end \cite{boiko2023autonomous}, while ChemCrow augments a language model with eighteen expert-designed tools to plan organic syntheses and guide discovery \cite{bran2024augmenting}. In materials science, physics-aware multi-agent systems such as AtomAgents integrate knowledge retrieval, multimodal data, and physics-based simulation to address alloy-design tasks \cite{ghafarollahi2024atomagents}. A common thread is that the scientific value emerges not from any single model but from an \emph{orchestrated ensemble of specialized, tool-backed capabilities}---precisely the composition that magnetic-domain analysis currently lacks.

\paragraph{Interoperability protocols for tool-augmented agents.}
For agents to reuse analytical capabilities safely, those capabilities must be exposed through a standardized interface rather than bespoke glue code. The Model Context Protocol (MCP) has recently emerged as an open standard that exposes tools and data sources to language-model agents through a uniform, auditable client--server contract, reducing the fragmentation that previously required a custom connector per tool \cite{hou2025mcp}. Such protocols turn heterogeneous scripts into \emph{composable capabilities} that external orchestrators can discover and invoke, but they have so far been applied to general-purpose software, web services, and databases rather than to physics-specific simulation and inference pipelines.

\paragraph{Knowledge representation and reusability in materials science.}
In parallel, the materials community has recognized that fragmented data systems, non-standardized formats, and the absence of shared metadata and ontologies are central obstacles to interoperability and reproducibility \cite{brinson2023community}. Efforts toward shared metadata schemas and semantic taxonomies aim to make data and workflows findable, interoperable, and reusable across groups \cite{ghiringhelli2023shared}. These initiatives target the \emph{data} layer; they do not yet expose executable physical models---simulators, estimators, generators, interpreters---as queryable, composable capabilities.

\paragraph{Research gap.}
Taken together, autonomous agents, interoperability protocols, and FAIR knowledge representation are advancing quickly, but they cluster in chemistry, general materials informatics, and robotics. No agent-orientable, physically grounded substrate exists for nanoscale magnetic-domain analysis, where the tooling remains bespoke and expert-operated, and where the forward simulation, inverse estimation, generative, and interpretability modules are neither standardized nor connected to one another. Unifying these modules into a single, coherent framework that can be reused---including by autonomous agents---is therefore an open problem, and constitutes the integrative contribution pursued in this work.

\section{Research Question}

The challenges outlined in the previous sections—parameter degeneracy, the sim-to-real gap, the lack of interpretability, and the fragmentation of the resulting methods into non-reusable, expert-operated tools—converge to define a critical gap in the state of the art for nanoscale magnetic domain characterization. Current approaches struggle to simultaneously address the non-uniqueness of the inverse mapping, the systematic distribution shifts between simulated training data and experimental observations, and the opacity of data-driven models, and even when individual solutions exist they remain isolated rather than combined into a coherent workflow. This confluence of limitations motivates the central research question of this work:

\begin{center}
\textit{How can a unified, physics-informed framework be designed to jointly mitigate parameter degeneracy, bridge the sim-to-real gap through domain adaptation, and ensure physical interpretability across the forward and inverse modeling of magnetic domain states, in a form reusable by autonomous agents?}
\end{center}

This question is inherently multifaceted, requiring the integration of regularization strategies that respect the underlying Hamiltonian structure, domain adaptation methodologies capable of generalizing from synthetic to experimental data while preserving physically plausible solutions, interpretability mechanisms that expose the relationship between learned representations and observable physical quantities, and, finally, the unification of these methods into a single, coherent framework that can be reused rather than rebuilt for each new study. Addressing this research question requires moving beyond purely data-driven approaches toward hybrid frameworks that embed physical constraints, leverage domain adaptation techniques, maintain computational tractability and generalization capacity across experimental and simulated magnetic imaging modalities, and remain accessible enough that autonomous agents can reuse the complete workflow.


\section{Objectives}

\subsection{General Objective}

Develop a physics-informed deep learning framework to simultaneously mitigate parameter degeneracy, bridge the sim-to-real gap through domain adaptation, and ensure physical interpretability in the forward and inverse modeling of magnetic domain states, and unify the resulting methods into a single, coherent framework that autonomous agents can reuse.

\subsection{Specific Objectives}

To achieve the general objective stated above, this work pursues four complementary specific objectives that tackle the core challenges of degeneracy, sim-to-real gap, interpretability, and the integration of the resulting methods in the modeling of magnetic domain states:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Develop a cycle-consistent physics-informed deep learning framework for bidirectional mapping between Hamiltonian parameters and magnetic domain configurations, employing physically informed regularization to mitigate the effects of magnetic state degeneracy.

    \item Develop a regularized deep learning framework based on variational methodologies to generate consistent magnetic states from specified conditions, incorporating domain-aware training strategies to ensure validity across both synthetic and experimental imaging modalities.

    \item Develop interpretability methodologies for deep learning models based on post-hoc attribution methods to identify spatially relevant features associated with the underlying conditions of magnetic domain states.

    \item Integrate the simulation, estimation, generation, and interpretation methods into a single, reproducible framework exposed through a standardized interface, enabling the resulting magnetic-domain workflow to be queried and reused programmatically and, as a proof of concept, accessed by autonomous (LLM-based) agents, rather than rebuilt for each new study.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Databases}\label{sec:databases}

This section describes the magnetic domain imaging datasets employed in this research, encompassing both synthetically generated training data and experimental reference datasets. The synthetic datasets provide controlled, physics-based ground truth for model development, while the experimental datasets enable validation of sim-to-real transfer capabilities and provide realistic imaging conditions for domain adaptation strategies.

\subsection{Hamiltonian-Based Synthetic Dataset Generation}\label{sec:synthetic_dataset}

This section presents the physics-based pipeline used to construct the synthetic dataset employed in this work. It couples an atomistic extended Heisenberg Hamiltonian with Metropolis--Hastings Monte Carlo sampling and simulated annealing to generate thermodynamically relaxed two-dimensional magnetization images, each labelled with its generating physical parameters.

\subsubsection{Atomistic Hamiltonian Framework}\label{sec:hamiltonian}

%hamiltonian fundaments
The theoretical foundation for modeling interacting magnetic dipole moments originates from the seminal formulations of Ernst Ising and Werner Heisenberg~\cite{agudelo2019study}. Ising introduced a one-dimensional statistical mechanics model wherein localized spins were heavily constrained to discrete, scalar states (i.e., rigidly pointing ``up'' or ``down''). While the Ising model provided early fundamental insights into macroscopic ferromagnetism and phase transitions, its strict uni-axial constraint renders it structurally insufficient for capturing the continuous rotational degrees of freedom required to model complex, multi-dimensional magnetic textures. This limitation was subsequently resolved by Heisenberg, who formulated a generalized model allowing spin vectors to vary continuously in three-dimensional space with rotational symmetry. While the foundational 
Heisenberg model successfully captures isotropic exchange driven by the Pauli exclusion principle, modern nanoscale applications—such as the study of magnetic vortices and topologically protected skyrmions in confined nanodot geometries—necessitate an extended 
approach~\cite{fert2024electrical}. This extended framework rigorously incorporates symmetry-breaking relativistic spin-orbit coupling effects, primarily the DMI and magnetocrystalline anisotropy, alongside standard Zeeman coupling~\cite{borisov2021heisenberg}.

%generalized formulation
Formally, let $\mathcal{V} = \{1, \dots, N\}$ denote the set of indices for the discrete magnetic lattice sites. The thermodynamic state of the system is characterized by the spatial distribution of localized magnetic moments, represented by the normalized spin vectors $\mathbf{s}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$, subject to the constraint 
$\|\mathbf{s}_i\|_2 = 1$, for $i\in\mathcal{V}$. In a generalized theoretical context, all pairwise symmetric and antisymmetric interactions, as well as site-specific field and anisotropy alignments, can be collapsed into a unified geometric approach~\cite{moriya1960anisotropic,eriksson2017atomistic}. The total effective energy of the system $\mathcal{H}\in\mathbb{R}$ [meV] is thus defined by the following generalized quadratic form:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:GeneralizedHamiltonian}
\mathcal{H}\left(\mathbf{S}| \breve{\theta}\right) = 
- \sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j\in \mathcal{N}(i)} 
    \langle \mathbf{s}_i, \tilde{\mathbf{M}}_{ij}\mathbf{s}_j \rangle
- \sum_{i=1}^{N} \langle\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{i},\mathbf{s}_i\rangle 
- \sum_{i=1}^{N} \langle\mathbf{s}_i \tilde{\mathbf{K}}_i, 
    \mathbf{s}_i\rangle,
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{N}(i)$ defines the set of interacting nearest neighbors for a given site $i$, and the matrix $\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 3}$ 
gathers the continuous spin vectors across the lattice with 
$\mathbf{s}_i\in\mathbf{S}$. Here, 
$\breve{\theta}=\{\tilde{\mathbf{M}}_{ij},\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{i},
\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{i}\}$, where 
$\tilde{\mathbf{M}}_{ij} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$ represents the complete bilinear interaction matrix coupling neighboring spins, $\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{3}$ accounts for linear external field interactions, and $\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$ 
is the anisotropy tensor governing site-specific directional preferences.

%Decomposition and Factorization
Afterward, to recover the isolated physical phenomena governed by the scalar Hamiltonian constants
$\tilde{J}_1,\tilde{J}_2,\tilde{J}_3,\tilde{J}_4,\tilde{K}_{DM},\tilde{H}_{ex},\tilde{K}_{an1},\tilde{K}_{anS}$, the generalized matrices must be mathematically decomposed. By factorizing these parameters, we can map each matrix operation to its specific energetic contribution:

\begin{itemize}

    \item[--] \emph{Bilinear Coupling ($\tilde{\mathbf{M}}_{ij}$):} A 
    real square matrix can be separated into its symmetric and skew-symmetric components. Here, 
    $\tilde{\mathbf{M}}_{ij}$ is parameterized by factorizing the isotropic exchange scalar $\tilde{J}_r$ for the $r$-th neighbor shell and the DMI magnitude $\tilde{K}_{DM}$ along a predefined spatial direction vector $\hat{\mathbf{d}}_{ij} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ 
    ($\|\hat{\mathbf{d}}_{ij}\|_2=1$), yielding:
    
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:MatrixDecomposition}
    \tilde{\mathbf{M}}_{ij}(\tilde{J}_r,\tilde{K}_{DM}) = 
        \tilde{J}_r \mathbf{I} - \tilde{K}_{DM} [\hat{\mathbf{d}}_{ij}]_{\times},
    \end{equation}
    where $\mathbf{I} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$ represents the identity matrix and 
    $[\hat{\mathbf{d}}_{ij}]_{\times} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$ denotes the skew-symmetric matrix representation of the cross-product. The symmetric exchange is modeled across four neighbor shells $r \in \{1,2,3,4\}$, where $\mathcal{N}_r(i)$ denotes the 
    set of neighbors at shell $r$ around site $i$, ordered by 
    increasing interatomic distance ($d_1 < d_2 < d_3 < d_4$). To resolve the fundamental scale ambiguity---whereby multiplying all parameters by a common factor leaves the observable spin configuration unchanged---the first-shell exchange is fixed as the energy reference unit, $\tilde{J}_1\in \mathbb{R}$ in meV, while $\tilde{J}_2, \tilde{J}_3, \tilde{J}_4 \in \mathbb{R}$ are free parameters (also in meV). The DMI acts exclusively between first-shell neighbors and is therefore indexed only over $\mathcal{N}_1(i)$.

    \item[--] \emph{Zeeman Interaction ($\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_i$):} The linear field term is factorized by extracting the scalar field magnitude $\tilde{H}_{ex}$ and the fixed normalized direction vector 
    $\hat{\mathbf{h}} = \hat{\mathbf{e}}_x,$ with 
    $\|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_x\|_2 = 1$, of the externally applied 
    magnetic field, yielding $\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_i = \tilde{H}_{ex}\,\hat{\mathbf{e}}_x\in\mathbb{R}^3$. Fixing the field direction along $\hat{\mathbf{e}}_x$ eliminates the orientational degree of freedom of $\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_i$, ensuring that $\tilde{H}_{ex}$ alone serves as the free parameter governing the Zeeman 
    contribution.

    \item[--] \emph{Magnetocrystalline Anisotropy 
    ($\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_i$):} Modeling cubic magnetocrystalline anisotropy requires factorizing a site-dependent scalar constant $\tilde{K}_i^{eff}$ to scale a quartic interaction. The principal crystallographic axes of the nanodot are defined by the orthonormal basis vectors $\mathbf{e}_{\hat{x}}, \mathbf{e}_{\hat{y}}, 
    \mathbf{e}_{\hat{z}} \in \mathbf{E}$, with $\mathbf{E} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$, and the directional cosines are given by $s_{im} = \langle \mathbf{e}_m,\mathbf{s}_i\rangle$ 
    ($\forall m\in\{\hat{x},\hat{y},\hat{z}\}$). Because atoms at the nanodot surface experience broken inversion symmetry---having fewer neighbors on the exterior side---their anisotropy differs fundamentally from that of interior atoms. The lattice is therefore partitioned into two disjoint subsets: the bulk region $\mathcal{V}_{bulk} \subset \mathcal{V}$, comprising interior sites with complete coordination shells, and the surface region 
    $\mathcal{V}_{surf} \subset \mathcal{V}$, comprising boundary sites exposed to the geometric confinement, with 
    $\mathcal{V}_{bulk} \cup \mathcal{V}_{surf} = \mathcal{V}$ and $\mathcal{V}_{bulk} \cap \mathcal{V}_{surf} = \emptyset$. This yields the site-dependent effective anisotropy constant:
    
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:AnisotropyEffective}
    \tilde{K}_i^{eff} = 
    \begin{cases}
        \tilde{K}_{an1} & \text{if } i \in \mathcal{V}_{bulk}, \\
        \tilde{K}_{anS} & \text{if } i \in \mathcal{V}_{surf},
    \end{cases}
    \end{equation}
    where $\tilde{K}_{an1}, \tilde{K}_{anS} \in \mathbb{R}$ are scalar constants measured in meV per atom~\cite{cullity2011introduction}.

\end{itemize}

%Explicit Formulation
Substituting these factorized relationships back into 
Equation~\ref{eq:GeneralizedHamiltonian} yields the following explicit formulation for the total extended Hamiltonian:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:Hamiltonian}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{H}\left(\mathbf{S}|\hat{\mathbf{d}}_{ij},\hat{\mathbf{e}}_x\right) =
&- \sum_{r=1}^{4}\tilde{J}_r
    \sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j\in \mathcal{N}_r(i)}
    \langle \mathbf{s}_i, \mathbf{s}_j \rangle + \tilde{K}_{DM} \sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j\in \mathcal{N}_1(i)}
    \langle \hat{\mathbf{d}}_{ij},(\mathbf{s}_i \times \mathbf{s}_j)
    \rangle \\
&- \tilde{H}_{ex} \sum_{i=1}^{N}
    \langle \hat{\mathbf{e}}_x, \mathbf{s}_i \rangle + \frac{1}{S_o^4}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \tilde{K}_i^{eff}
    \left(s_{i\hat{x}}^2 s_{i\hat{y}}^2 +
          s_{i\hat{x}}^2 s_{i\hat{z}}^2 +
          s_{i\hat{y}}^2 s_{i\hat{z}}^2\right),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
with $\tilde{J}_1 = 1.0$~meV fixed as the energy reference unit. The cubic anisotropy form in the last term follows directly from expanding $\frac{1}{2}(1 - \|\mathbf{s}_i\mathbf{E}\|_4^4)$ under the $L_2$ normalization constraint $\|\mathbf{s}_i\|_2 = 1$, which 
yields $s_{i\hat{x}}^2 s_{i\hat{y}}^2 + s_{i\hat{y}}^2 
s_{i\hat{z}}^2 + s_{i\hat{z}}^2 s_{i\hat{x}}^2$, normalized by $S_o^4$ to maintain dimensional consistency across spin magnitudes.

%parameter interpretation
Physically, this extended set of Hamiltonian constants actively governs the competing energetic contributions that shape the magnetic energy landscape. Each $\tilde{J}_r$ dictates the collinear alignment tendency between spin pairs separated by the corresponding distance~\cite{coey2010magnetism}: positive values ($\tilde{J}_r > 0$) favor parallel, ferromagnetic 
alignment, while negative values ($\tilde{J}_r < 0$) drive 
antiparallel, antiferromagnetic order. This multi-shell formulation is essential in confined nanodot geometries, where longer-range exchange pathways--mediated through intermediate atomic orbitals--contribute non-negligibly to the global energetic competition and can selectively stabilize or destabilize complex topological textures beyond what first-shell interactions alone would predict~\cite{eriksson2017atomistic}. Among these constants, $\tilde{J}_1$ plays a privileged role: it 
corresponds to the strongest and most spatially direct exchange pathway, coupling each spin to its nearest neighbors in the cubic lattice and establishing the dominant energy scale of the magnetic system. For this reason, $\tilde{J}_1$ is fixed to $1.0$~meV throughout all simulations, serving as the absolute energy reference unit against which all remaining interaction strengths are expressed. The remaining constants $\tilde{J}_2$, $\tilde{J}_3$, and $\tilde{J}_4$ are free parameters whose magnitudes decay with increasing shell distance, consistent with the exponential attenuation of quantum mechanical exchange integrals with interatomic separation~\cite{coey2010magnetism}.

The DMI magnitude, represented by $\tilde{K}_{DM}$, is responsible for driving chiral, non-collinear spin winding in systems exhibiting broken spatial inversion symmetry~\cite{fert2017magnetic}. By actively competing with the dominant first-shell exchange $\tilde{J}_1$, 
this antisymmetric contribution stabilizes complex topological textures, such as chiral domain walls and skyrmions. It acts exclusively between first-shell neighbors, as longer-range antisymmetric exchange pathways are negligibly small compared to the dominant spin-orbit coupling at nearest-neighbor interfaces. Measured in meV per interacting spin pair, its magnitude is fundamentally dependent on the strength of the spin-orbit coupling 
at the material's interfaces, with typical values spanning 
$[-0.5, 0.5]$~meV as a fraction of the first-shell exchange 
energy~\cite{fert2017magnetic}.

The Zeeman coupling energy, parameterized as $\tilde{H}_{ex}$, quantifies the interaction between the localized atomic spins and the externally applied magnetic field~\cite{evans2014atomistic}. It systematically 
penalizes magnetic misalignment with the fixed crystallographic axis $\hat{\mathbf{e}}_x$, frequently serving as the primary thermodynamic driving force for macroscopic magnetic phase transitions. This 
parameter is expressed in meV per atom, mathematically representing the effective energy equivalence ($g\mu_B B$) that transitions a macroscopic magnetic field $B$ measured in Tesla into the atomistic energy regime. Its boundaries range from $0.0$~meV to approximately $2.0$~meV, directly correlating to laboratory-scale magnetic fields 
between $0$ and $10$~Tesla. Also, fixing the field direction along $\hat{\mathbf{e}}_x$ reduces the dimensionality of the parameter space by eliminating the orientational degree of freedom of $\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_i$, ensuring that $\tilde{H}_{ex}$ alone governs the Zeeman contribution as a single identifiable scalar.

Finally, the magnetocrystalline anisotropy encodes the intrinsic directional preference for magnetization relative to the underlying crystal lattice~\cite{cullity2011introduction}. Arising from 
relativistic spin-orbit coupling, this term rigorously establishes the energetically favorable (easy) and unfavorable (hard) crystallographic axes for spin orientation within the nanodot. Both constants are measured in meV per atom and represent a relatively subtle symmetry-breaking energy compared to the dominant isotropic 
exchange. The bulk anisotropy constant $\tilde{K}_{an1}$ governs interior lattice sites $i \in \mathcal{V}_{bulk}$, where each atom is fully coordinated by neighbors in all spatial directions, yielding a locally symmetric energetic environment. Its conventional boundaries range from roughly $0.001$~meV in soft bulk magnets to $1.0$~meV in 
highly anisotropic hard nanomaterials~\cite{cullity2011introduction}. The surface anisotropy constant $\tilde{K}_{anS}$, by contrast, 
governs boundary sites $i \in \mathcal{V}_{surf}$, where broken spatial inversion symmetry---arising from the absence of neighbors on the exterior side of the nanodot---produces an asymmetric local environment that intensifies the directional preference for spin orientation. Consequently, $|\tilde{K}_{anS}|$ is generally larger than $|\tilde{K}_{an1}|$, reflecting the enhanced symmetry-breaking at the nanodot boundary. This two-constant formulation is essential for accurately capturing the finite-size and surface-dominated effects that critically govern the stability of emergent topological textures
in confined nanoscale geometries~\cite{fert2024electrical,borisov2021heisenberg}.

Although the parameter vector $\breve{\theta}$ governs the full three-dimensional spin field, the observable exploited in this work is restricted to its out-of-plane projection, the $s_z$ component. This choice is dictated by experimental measurability: the most widely available magnetic imaging modalities---magnetic force microscopy and the polar magneto-optical Kerr effect---are primarily sensitive to the out-of-plane magnetization~\cite{li2023skyrmions}, whereas resolving the in-plane components requires substantially more specialized techniques. Working on $s_z$ therefore keeps the framework consistent with the information realistically accessible from experiment. This restriction, however, imposes a theoretical limit on parameter identifiability: distinct parameter sets---most notably those related by the sign of the antisymmetric exchange, which reverses the in-plane chirality while leaving $s_z$ invariant---become indistinguishable in the observation space, so that a regressor exposed to both would collapse its predictions toward a degenerate average. The sampling bounds detailed in Section~\ref{sec:training_datasets} are therefore deliberately chosen to remain well-posed under the $s_z$-only observation.

\subsubsection{Image-Based Dataset Generation from Monte Carlo Simulations}\label{sec:montecarlo_sim}

To accurately capture the physical thermodynamic behavior and algorithmically isolate the global energy minimum (the ground state) of the nanodot, thermal fluctuations must be formally introduced through the framework of statistical mechanics. While the atomistic Hamiltonian $\mathcal{H}$ rigorously defines the internal magnetic energy for a given spatial configuration of spins, it evaluates the system strictly as a frozen, zero-temperature snapshot. Hence, temperature is fundamentally a macroscopic parameter that governs the statistical distribution of the discrete magnetic microstates rather than acting as a localized interaction within the Hamiltonian. Consequently, temperature $T$ $[K]$ couples to the system via the Boltzmann distribution~\cite{eriksson2017atomistic}. Formally, a magnetic microstate $\mu$ represents a specific, instantaneous realization of the continuous spin matrix, denoted as $\mathbf{S}_\mu$. The total thermodynamic energy of this specific configuration is evaluated against the static environmental vectors and physical interaction strengths, such that $\mathcal{H}(\mu) \equiv \mathcal{H}\left(\mathbf{S}_\mu|\mathbf{\hat{d}}_{ij},\hat{\mathbf{h}},\mathbf{E}\right)$. At thermal equilibrium, the probability $P(\mu)$ of the system occupying this specific microstate is defined as:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:Boltzmann}
P(\mu) = \frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}} \exp\left(-\frac{\mathcal{H}(\mu)}{k_B T}\right),
\end{equation}
where $k_B$ is the Boltzmann constant (expressed in $\text{meV/K}$). The term $\mathcal{Z}$ represents the canonical partition function, which mathematically enforces probability normalization by summing over all valid spin configurations $\nu$ (i.e., all possible state matrices $\mathbf{S}_\nu$ strictly satisfying the geometric boundary constraint $\|\mathbf{s}_i\|_2 = 1$):

\begin{equation}\label{eq:PartitionFunction}
\mathcal{Z} = \sum_{\mathbf{S}_\nu} \exp\left(-\frac{\mathcal{H}\left(\mathbf{S}_\nu|\mathbf{\hat{d}}_{ij},\hat{\mathbf{h}},\mathbf{E}\right)}{k_B T}\right).
\end{equation}

In our computational framework, this thermodynamic distribution is actively sampled using the Metropolis-Hastings Monte Carlo algorithm combined with a simulated annealing protocol~\cite{landau2021guide}. This stochastic sampling ensures a rigorous exploration of the highly multimodal energy landscape characteristic of these degenerate magnetic systems. When a localized spin perturbation proposes a physical transition from the current microstate $\mu^{(t)}$ (characterized by $\mathbf{S}_{\mu^{(t)}}$) to a newly modified state $\mu^{(t+1)}$ (characterized by $\mathbf{S}_{\mu^{(t+1)}}$), the resulting change in the system's total energy is computed as the scalar difference $\Delta \mathcal{H} = \mathcal{H}(\mu^{(t+1)}) - \mathcal{H}(\mu^{(t)})$. To optimize computational efficiency, $\Delta \mathcal{H}$ is evaluated locally at the perturbed lattice site. The proposed state is subsequently accepted or rejected based on the precise transition probability:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:Metropolis}
P(\mu^{(t)} \rightarrow \mu^{(t+1)}) = \begin{cases}
1 & \text{if } \Delta \mathcal{H} < 0 \\
\exp\left(-\frac{\Delta \mathcal{H}}{k_B T^{(t)}}\right) & \text{if } \Delta \mathcal{H} \geq 0.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

Physically, the instantaneous temperature parameter $T^{(t)}$ actively scales the energetic barriers within the system's vast topological energy landscape during the annealing process~\cite{landau2021guide}. At elevated temperatures, the available thermal energy $k_B T^{(t)}$ frequently exceeds local energy penalties ($\Delta \mathcal{H}$), allowing the simulation to accept thermodynamically unfavorable spin alignments. This thermal noise provides the critical kinetic mechanism required to break out of metastable, localized energy minima, such as misaligned ferromagnetic domains~\cite{desplat2018thermal}. As $T^{(t)}$ is systematically scaled down during the annealing procedure, the probability of accepting higher-energy configurations undergoes exponential decay. This controlled thermal relaxation forces the continuous spin vector field to settle into the absolute global minimum, successfully stabilizing the complex chiral skyrmion textures driven by the underlying DMI.

Moreover, to ensure that each simulation consistently originates from a fully randomized paramagnetic phase regardless of the specific parameter configuration, the initial annealing temperature (i.e., $T^{(t)}$ at $t=0$) is established as an \textit{exchange-normalized initial temperature}. Consistent with foundational statistical mechanics~\cite{eriksson2017atomistic}, this starting temperature is dynamically rescaled in direct proportion to the dominant symmetric exchange energy ($\tilde{J}$). This energetic normalization guarantees that the initial thermal fluctuations are strictly sufficient to overcome the highest localized energetic barriers ($\Delta \mathcal{H}$) for that specific Hamiltonian. This effectively erases initial state bias and prevents the continuous spin vector field from prematurely collapsing into metastable local minima before the controlled cooling protocol begins. Figure~\ref{fig:convergence} illustrates this annealing process for a representative nanodot, tracking the total Hamiltonian energy as the system is cooled and visualizing the progressive transition from a disordered paramagnetic phase toward a thermodynamically relaxed domain configuration.

The stochastic sampling described above is realized as a fully vectorized, GPU-resident computation, which constitutes one of the core methodological contributions targeted by this work. Leveraging the matrix formulation of the Hamiltonian (Section~\ref{sec:hamiltonian}), the local energy variation $\Delta\mathcal{H}$ is not evaluated site by site---as in conventional atomistic simulators---but for the entire spin lattice at once, gathering neighbor contributions through tensor-shift operations combined as batched matrix and tensor products in a single pass. As illustrated in the bottom inset of Figure~\ref{fig:methodology_flowchart}, this parallelization operates on three levels, each acting only on independent or conditionally-independent degrees of freedom: \textbf{(a)}~\emph{spins}---the lattice is split into two interpenetrating sublattices through a checkerboard decomposition ($(x+y+z)\bmod 2$), so that, because the nearest-neighbor Hamiltonian makes the two sublattices conditionally independent, all sites of one sublattice are proposed and accepted simultaneously (with the other held fixed) before switching, exactly reproducing a sequential sweep; \textbf{(b)}~\emph{replicas}---the same parameter set $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ is propagated with different random seeds as independent Markov chains, batched as an additional dimension on a single GPU; and \textbf{(c)}~\emph{experiments}---different parameter sets $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ are distributed across multiple GPUs under a single-program multiple-data (\texttt{pmap}) scheme, and the complete annealing trajectory is just-in-time compiled and executed on-device to eliminate host--device transfers at every sweep. Crucially, the annealing temperature schedule and the Monte Carlo sweeps $\mu^{(t)}\!\to\!\mu^{(t+1)}$ remain strictly sequential, so this massively parallel formulation reduces the wall-clock cost of dataset generation by orders of magnitude relative to sequential atomistic codes \emph{without introducing any approximation to the underlying physics}.

To generate a robust and diverse dataset, the magnetic parameters governing the Hamiltonian were systematically varied across the simulations. Key variables included the exchange interaction constant ($\tilde{J}$), the magnetocrystalline anisotropy ($\tilde{K}$), the external Zeeman field ($\tilde{H}_{ex}$), and the DMI strength ($\tilde{K}_{DM}$), alongside variations in the base initial temperature ($T^{(0)}$). Subsequently, the transformation of these raw, converged 3D spin states into normalized 2D image arrays followed a strict procedural pipeline:

\begin{itemize}
    \item[--] \emph{Geometry parsing:} Spatial coordinates defining the nanodot geometry (with a fixed disk radius and thickness as Magnetic Unit Cell (MUC) measure) were established using a binary circular lattice mask.
    \item[--] \emph{Spin state mapping:} The topological structure was projected into two dimensions by extracting the out-of-plane $s_z$ component of the continuous spin vectors ($\mathbf{s}_i$) across the valid lattice coordinates. 
    \item[--] \emph{Image rendering:} The isolated configurations were rasterized onto a fixed $\breve{H} \times \breve{W}$ pixel grid. A divergent red--blue colormap was utilized to strictly encode opposite spin polarities, visually capturing the chiral winding from the nanodot's core to its perimeter.
    \item[--] \emph{Preprocessing:} Pixel intensities corresponding to the $S_z$ magnitude were normalized to a strict interval $[0, 1]$ to ensure a consistent numerical range across all samples. To preserve the intrinsic physical symmetries and chiral handedness dictated by the DMI, no spatial augmentations (such as random rotations or structural flips) were applied to the dataset.
\end{itemize}

It is worth noting that, although the dataset records all eight free parameters for every sample, their physical imprint on the $s_z$ projection differs substantially. The higher-order exchange constants $\tilde{J}_3$ and $\tilde{J}_4$ are energetically dominated by the first- and second-shell interactions, while the anisotropy constants $\tilde{K}_{an1}$, $\tilde{K}_{anS}$ and the external field $\tilde{H}_{ex}$ couple primarily to the in-plane spin components $(s_x, s_y)$, which are not captured by the $s_z$ projection extracted from the central layer. They are nonetheless included in the per-sample labels to provide a complete and physically honest characterization of the simulated configurations.

To broaden the structural diversity of the dataset beyond a single field geometry, the external field orientation is varied across the simulations: in addition to the in-plane configuration, an out-of-plane field is employed to stabilize additional topologies such as isolated skyrmions, while the magnetocrystalline anisotropy range is deliberately extended toward high values to access an Ising-like regime characterized by sharper, more discrete domain walls. This last point directly probes the transition between the continuous-symmetry (Heisenberg) and discrete ($\mathbb{Z}_2$ Ising) regimes: by driving the uniaxial anisotropy to large values, the continuous spins are progressively forced toward two preferred orientations, allowing the same framework to span from smooth chiral textures to abrupt Ising-like boundaries. The dataset also spans two complementary mechanisms for stabilizing modulated, non-collinear textures: a chiral regime driven by the DMI $\tilde{K}_{DM}$ and an exchange-frustration regime driven by the second-shell exchange $\tilde{J}_2$. To isolate the contribution of each mechanism, these two interactions are activated in a mutually exclusive fashion across the sampled configurations, so that $\tilde{J}_2$ and $\tilde{K}_{DM}$ are the only pair of parameters exhibiting an appreciable anti-correlation ($\rho \approx -0.53$), all remaining pairs being essentially uncorrelated ($|\rho| \lesssim 0.3$). This controlled heterogeneity enriches the space of magnetic phases the models must disentangle within a single unified dataset; Figure~\ref{fig:dataset_samples} shows a representative image for each of the five phase categories the dataset spans, from ordered helical stripes and dense skyrmion lattices to sharp Ising-like domain walls.

It is important to note that the DMI strength $\tilde{K}_{DM}$ is sampled exclusively over non-negative values ($\tilde{K}_{DM} \in [0.0, 1.2]$~meV). This restriction is a physically motivated convention arising from the symmetry of the antisymmetric exchange term in Equation~\ref{eq:Hamiltonian}: under the transformation $\tilde{K}_{DM} \to -\tilde{K}_{DM}$, the equilibrium spin configuration reverses its in-plane chirality---the rotational sense of the chiral winding inverts---while the out-of-plane component $s_z$ at every lattice site remains strictly invariant. Since the regression models operate exclusively on the $s_z$ projection, configurations with $+\tilde{K}_{DM}$ and $-\tilde{K}_{DM}$ are exactly degenerate in the observation space, rendering the sign of the DMI fundamentally unidentifiable from $s_z$ images alone; including both signs would collapse the model's predictions toward $\hat{\tilde{K}}_{DM} \approx 0$. The chirality handedness is instead encoded through the fixed direction vector $\hat{\mathbf{d}}_{ij}$, consistent with standard conventions in atomistic modeling of interfacial DMI~\cite{fert2017magnetic, bogdanov1989thermodynamically, fillion2022gate}. Recovering the DMI sign would require chirality-sensitive observables, such as the full three-dimensional spin field or in-plane magnetization components; this lies outside the scope of the present work and is left as a direction for future investigation.

Finally, the sampling bounds of Table~\ref{tab:param_ranges} are not intended to reproduce a single specific compound but to cover the region of Hamiltonian space where chiral and modulated textures exist. Since $\tilde{J}_1$ is fixed as the energy reference, the physically meaningful quantities are the dimensionless ratios $\tilde{J}_r/\tilde{J}_1$, $\tilde{K}_{DM}/\tilde{J}_1$, and $\tilde{K}/\tilde{J}_1$, which fall within the ranges reported by first-principles calculations and experiments for chiral magnets and ultrathin transition-metal films~\cite{heinze2011spontaneous, dupe2014tailoring}. For the DMI, the sampled ratio $\tilde{K}_{DM}/\tilde{J}_1$ up to $\approx 1.2$ exceeds the typical interfacial value ($\sim 0.3$--$0.7$), a standard choice in finite-lattice Monte Carlo so that the helical period ($\lambda \propto \tilde{J}_1/\tilde{K}_{DM}$) fits several times within the simulation cell; because the physics is scale-invariant, a large ratio on a small lattice is equivalent to a small ratio in a large system, reflecting finite-size rescaling rather than unphysical values~\cite{buhrandt2013skyrmion}. The anisotropy range spans from soft magnets to the strongly anisotropic interfacial regime~\cite{gambardella2003giant, johnson1996magnetic}, and the Zeeman range ($\tilde{H}_{ex}$ up to $1.2$~meV/atom) corresponds, through $E = g\mu_B B$ with $g\approx2$, to laboratory fields up to $\approx 10$~T.

\section{Proposed Methodology Framework}\label{sec:proposed_framework}

This section presents the integrated methodological framework proposed to address the three research objectives defined in Section 5. The framework combines physics-informed deep learning architectures, cycle-consistent regularization, domain adaptation strategies, and interpretability mechanisms into a coherent pipeline. Figure~\ref{fig:proposed_pipeline} illustrates the complete methodology, with each objective highlighted in a distinct color to indicate which components of the pipeline contribute to its fulfillment.

The proposed framework is structured around three interconnected stages:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Cycle-Consistent Bidirectional Mapping (Objective 1 - Degeneracy Mitigation):} This stage couples two complementary pathways. The first is an inverse parameter estimator based on transfer learning from pretrained CNNs (DenseNet, ResNet, EfficientNet) that maps magnetic domain images to Hamiltonian parameters. The second is a forward conditional generative model that synthesizes the magnetic domain image from the Hamiltonian parameters. Rather than relying on a single generator, we consider a family of conditional generative architectures --- conditional variational autoencoders (cVAE), denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPM), and U-Net--based generators --- all conditioned on the physical parameters and optionally regularized with a physics-based term during training. The two pathways are closed into a complete evaluation cycle: from a given set of parameters the forward model generates an image, and the inverse model attempts to recover the original parameters from that generated image. Assessing the full cycle at the image level (e.g., MSE, SSIM, Fourier-domain similarity), at the physical level (magnetization, susceptibility, specific heat, and energy computed on the nanodot), and at the parameter level (input versus re-estimated) lets the framework constrain the intrinsic degeneracy of the inverse problem and steer it toward a single, physically consistent solution.

    \item \textbf{Domain Adaptation for Sim-to-Real Transfer (Objective 2):} The forward generative model is extended with domain adaptation strategies to enable generalization from synthetic training data to experimental imaging conditions. This includes data-level augmentation to simulate realistic noise and artifacts, and feature-level alignment to match latent representations between simulated and experimental domains.

    \item \textbf{Interpretability and Physical Validation (Objective 3):} Post-hoc attribution methods (Grad-CAM, Integrated Gradients, occlusion analysis) combined with physics-guided constraints provide transparency into model decisions. Regression activation maps (RAMs) and latent space analysis (UMAP) enable quantitative assessment of spatial relevance and representational structure, ensuring that learned features align with physical principles.
\end{enumerate}

Bayesian hyperparameter optimization orchestrates the trade-offs between reconstruction fidelity, physics consistency, inverse accuracy, cycle coherence, and domain adaptation, systematically exploring the hyperparameter landscape to identify configurations that maximize performance across all objectives. \section{Methodology}\label{sec:Material_methods}

This section describes the methodological framework adopted to address the research objectives outlined in Section 5. The methodology integrates physics-informed deep learning architectures enhanced with cycle-consistent regularization, Bayesian hyperparameter optimization, domain adaptation strategies for sim-to-real transfer, and interpretability mechanisms for physical validation. The synthetic datasets described in Section~\ref{sec:databases} provide the controlled foundation for model development, while the experimental reference datasets guide domain adaptation and validation.

\subsection{Specific Objective 1: Cycle-Consistent Bidirectional Mapping with Degeneracy Mitigation}

This subsection addresses the first specific objective: developing a cycle-consistent physics-informed deep learning framework for bidirectional mapping between Hamiltonian parameters and magnetic domain configurations. The methodology integrates transfer learning for inverse parameter estimation, conditional variational autoencoders for forward generation, cycle-consistent constraints coupling both directions, and Bayesian hyperparameter optimization to ensure physically plausible generation and statistically robust parameter estimation while mitigating degeneracy effects.

\subsubsection{Deep Regression Framework for Hamiltonian Parameter Estimation}\label{sec:deep_regression}

The inverse estimation of the Hamiltonian parameters and thermodynamic state from magnetic domain images is formalized as a continuous mapping problem. Given the generated dataset $\mathcal{D}$, where $\mathbf{X}_n$ represents the normalized $s_z$ spatial magnetization field and $\boldsymbol{\theta}_n$ represents the vector of generating physical parameters, we aim to learn a parameterized non-linear mapping function $f_\Theta : \mathbb{R}^{\breve{H} \times \breve{W}} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{\breve{P}}$, to find the predicted parameters $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_n\in\mathbb{R}^{\breve{P}}.$

Here, $f_\Theta$ denotes a deep neural network architecture parameterized by a set of learnable weights and biases $\Theta$. The optimal parameter set $\hat{\Theta}$ is obtained by minimizing a continuous loss function over the training distribution~\cite{murphy2023probabilistic}:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:LossFunction}
\hat{\Theta} = \arg\min_{\Theta} \frac{1}{\breve{N}} \sum_{n=1}^{\breve{N}} \mathcal{L}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}_n, f_\Theta(\mathbf{X}_n)\right),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}$ represents the chosen regression objective, typically the Mean Squared Error (MSE) or Huber loss, which mitigates the influence of outlier topological states during optimization. By minimizing this objective, the network intrinsically learns to untangle the highly multimodal spatial features mapping to the specific energetic balances of the extended Heisenberg Hamiltonian.

To achieve robust feature extraction across varying scales of magnetic textures (e.g., from localized domain walls to global skyrmion geometries), this framework evaluates four distinct, state-of-the-art deep learning architectures: ResNet50, DenseNet, Xception, and the Visual Transformer (ViT).

\begin{itemize}

\item[--] \emph{Residual Networks (ResNet50)}~\cite{koonce2021resnet}: Standard deep convolutional networks often suffer from the vanishing gradient problem as depth increases, hindering the learning of complex physical mappings. ResNet50 circumvents this by introducing shortcut connections that bypass multiple convolutional layers, directly propagating spatial information and gradients. Mathematically, if $\mathbf{x}$ is the input to a sequence of layers, and $\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}, \{\mathbf{W}_l\})$ represents the residual mapping to be learned (comprising convolutions, batch normalization, and ReLU activations) for the $l$-th layer, the output $\mathbf{y}$ of the residual block is defined as:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ResNet}
\mathbf{y} = \mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}, \{\mathbf{W}_l\}) + \mathbf{x}.
\end{equation}

In the context of magnetic domain images, these skip connections allow the network to simultaneously preserve low-level spatial features (like abrupt chiral boundaries) while composing deep, high-level abstractions of the global thermodynamic state (see Figure~\ref{fig:resnet_implementation}).

\begin{equation}\label{eq:DenseNet}
\mathbf{x}_l = H_l([\mathbf{x}_0, \mathbf{x}_1, \dots, \mathbf{x}_{l-1}]),
\end{equation}
where $[\dots]$ denotes the concatenation operation. This dense topological structure ensures that the final regression head has direct access to both fine-grained pixel interactions and coarse global structures (see Figure~\ref{fig:densenet_implementation}).

\item[--] \emph{Xception (Extreme Inception)}~\cite{chollet2017xception}: This architecture operates on the hypothesis that cross-channel correlations and spatial correlations in feature maps can be entirely decoupled. It replaces standard Inception modules with depthwise separable convolutions, drastically reducing computational overhead while enhancing representational efficiency. A depthwise separable convolution splits the standard convolution into two distinct steps. First, a depthwise convolution applies a single spatial filter to each input channel independently. Second, a pointwise convolution ($1 \times 1$ convolution) projects the output of the depthwise step onto a new channel space to capture cross-channel correlations. If $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{\breve{H} \times \breve{W} \times \breve{C}}$ is the input tensor, the operation is formally approximated as:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:Xception}
\text{Conv}_{\text{sep}}(\mathbf{X}) = \text{Conv}_{\text{pointwise}}(\text{Conv}_{\text{depthwise}}(\mathbf{X})).
\end{equation}

For nanoscale magnetism, this extreme decoupling allows the network to efficiently isolate specific topological frequency patterns (depthwise) before correlating them to the complex parameter space $\boldsymbol{\theta}_n$ (see Figure~\ref{fig:xception_implementation}).

\item[--] \emph{Visual Transformer (ViT)}~\cite{dosovitskiy2020image}: Unlike Convolutional Neural Networks, which enforce a strict local inductive bias, the Vision Transformer processes the 2D spatial magnetization field $\mathbf{X}_n$ as a sequence of flattened, non-overlapping 2D patches. This allows the model to capture long-range, global dependencies across the nanodot instantly, which is critical for understanding macro-scale topological stability dictated by the Zeeman field or global anisotropy. The input image is divided into $N_p$ patches, which are linearly projected into a sequence of $D$-dimensional embeddings. The core mechanism is the Multi-Head Self-Attention (MSA), which computes attention scores to weigh the importance of different patches relative to one another. For a given set of queries $Q$, keys $K$, and values $V$ derived from the patch embeddings, the scaled dot-product attention is defined as:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ViT}
\text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V,
\end{equation}
where $d_k$ is the scaling factor (the dimension of the keys). By employing MSA, the ViT can explicitly correlate a localized structural defect on one edge of the nanodot with a compensatory chiral boundary on the opposite edge, mapping these distant correlations directly to the global DMI or symmetric exchange strengths.

The optimization process is executed using the Adam optimizer, relying on backpropagation and automatic differentiation to efficiently compute the network gradients~\cite{murakami2022inverse}. By iteratively updating the model weights based on these computed gradients, the training pipeline systematically minimizes the structural and energetic discrepancies between the predicted outputs and the true Hamiltonian constants, ensuring robust convergence in estimating the underlying physical parameters.

\paragraph{Integration into the Cycle-Consistent Framework.}
This deep regression model constitutes the inverse pathway $f_{\hat{\Theta}}$ of the cycle-consistent framework (Section~\ref{sec:cycle_consistency}), where it is coupled with the forward conditional generative model. By enforcing round-trip parameter consistency --- recovering the Hamiltonian parameters from the generated magnetic state --- the framework constrains the intrinsic degeneracy of the inverse problem and steers it toward a single, physically consistent solution.

\subsubsection{Conditional Generative Models for the Forward Map}\label{sec:cvae_forward}

The forward pathway learns the conditional generative map $g_{\Phi}:\boldsymbol{\theta}\mapsto\mathbf{X}$, which synthesizes a magnetic domain image $\mathbf{X}\in[0,1]^{\breve{H}\times\breve{W}}$ from a Hamiltonian parameter vector $\boldsymbol{\theta}$, thereby inverting the direction of the regression model $f_{\hat{\Theta}}$ of Section~\ref{sec:deep_regression}. Because the forward map is intrinsically one-to-many --- at finite temperature, many thermally relaxed textures are compatible with a given $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ --- it is modeled probabilistically as $p_{\Phi}(\mathbf{X}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\theta})$. We consider two complementary families of conditional generators, both conditioned on the physical parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$: a conditional variational autoencoder (cVAE) and a conditional denoising diffusion model (DDPM).

\paragraph{Conditional Variational Autoencoder (cVAE).}
The cVAE introduces a latent variable $\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_z}$ and couples three networks: a posterior (encoder) $q(\mathbf{z}\,|\,\mathbf{X},\boldsymbol{\theta})$, a learned conditional prior $p(\mathbf{z}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\theta})$, and a decoder that reconstructs $\hat{\mathbf{X}}$ from $(\mathbf{z},\boldsymbol{\theta})$. The encoder reuses one of the pretrained inverse backbones of Section~\ref{sec:deep_regression} (Xception or ViT-B/16, kept partially frozen), whose pooled features are concatenated with a learned embedding of $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ to produce a diagonal Gaussian posterior,
\begin{equation}
q(\mathbf{z}\,|\,\mathbf{X},\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \mathcal{N}\!\big(\mathbf{z};\,\boldsymbol{\mu}_q(\mathbf{X},\boldsymbol{\theta}),\,\mathrm{diag}\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_q^2(\mathbf{X},\boldsymbol{\theta})\big),\qquad
\mathbf{z} = \boldsymbol{\mu}_q + \boldsymbol{\sigma}_q\odot\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\quad \boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}),
\label{eq:cvae_posterior}
\end{equation}
where the second expression is the reparameterization used for low-variance gradient estimation. Unlike the classical VAE, which fixes an uninformative prior $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})$, the latent space is structured by a \emph{learned conditional prior} driven by the physical parameters,
\begin{equation}
p(\mathbf{z}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \mathcal{N}\!\big(\mathbf{z};\,\boldsymbol{\mu}_p(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\,\mathrm{diag}\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_p^2(\boldsymbol{\theta})\big),
\label{eq:cvae_prior}
\end{equation}
so that distinct parameter regimes are mapped to distinct latent regions, discouraging the collapse of physically different states into overlapping codes. The model is trained by minimizing a reconstruction term --- combining structural similarity (SSIM) and an $L_1$ penalty computed between the target and reconstructed images --- regularized by the Kullback--Leibler (KL) divergence between posterior and conditional prior,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cVAE}} = \underbrace{\big(1-\mathrm{SSIM}(\mathbf{X},\hat{\mathbf{X}})\big) + \lambda_{L_1}\,\lVert \mathbf{X}-\hat{\mathbf{X}}\rVert_1}_{\text{reconstruction}} \;+\; \beta\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(q(\mathbf{z}\,|\,\mathbf{X},\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\|\,p(\mathbf{z}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\theta})\big).
\label{eq:cvae_total_loss}
\end{equation}
For the two diagonal Gaussians the KL term admits the closed form
\begin{equation}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(q\,\|\,p\big) = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{d_z}\left[\log\frac{\sigma_{p,k}^2}{\sigma_{q,k}^2} + \frac{\sigma_{q,k}^2 + (\mu_{q,k}-\mu_{p,k})^2}{\sigma_{p,k}^2} - 1\right].
\label{eq:cvae_kl}
\end{equation}
The weight $\beta$ follows a warmup--plateau schedule (annealed from a near-zero value to a fixed $\beta_{\max}$) to prevent posterior collapse. At inference, generation is purely conditional: a latent code is drawn from the prior, $\mathbf{z}\sim p(\mathbf{z}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\theta})$, and decoded together with $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ to yield $\hat{\mathbf{X}}$. \paragraph{Conditional Denoising Diffusion Model (DDPM).}
The diffusion model defines a fixed forward process that gradually corrupts a clean image $\mathbf{x}_0=\mathbf{X}$ with Gaussian noise over $T$ steps according to a variance schedule $\{\beta_t\}_{t=1}^{T}$ (a cosine schedule is used). Writing $\alpha_t = 1-\beta_t$ and $\bar{\alpha}_t = \prod_{s=1}^{t}\alpha_s$, the marginal at step $t$ has the closed form
\begin{equation}
q(\mathbf{x}_t\,|\,\mathbf{x}_0) = \mathcal{N}\!\big(\mathbf{x}_t;\,\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0,\,(1-\bar{\alpha}_t)\mathbf{I}\big),\qquad
\mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\;\;\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}).
\label{eq:ddpm_forward}
\end{equation}
A conditional U-Net $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\Phi}(\mathbf{x}_t,t,\boldsymbol{\theta})$ is trained to predict the injected noise given the noisy image, the timestep $t$, and the physical parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$, by minimizing the denoising objective
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DDPM}} = \mathbb{E}_{t,\,\mathbf{x}_0,\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\Big[\,\big\lVert \boldsymbol{\epsilon} - \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\Phi}(\mathbf{x}_t,t,\boldsymbol{\theta})\big\rVert^2\,\Big],\qquad t\sim\mathcal{U}\{1,\dots,T\}.
\label{eq:ddpm_loss}
\end{equation}
New magnetic states are produced by reversing the process: starting from pure noise $\mathbf{x}_T\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})$ and iterating, for $t=T,\dots,1$,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}_{t-1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\left(\mathbf{x}_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\Phi}(\mathbf{x}_t,t,\boldsymbol{\theta})\right) + \sigma_t\,\mathbf{z},\qquad \mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}),
\label{eq:ddpm_sampling}
\end{equation}
which yields the generated image $\hat{\mathbf{X}}=\mathbf{x}_0$ conditioned on $\boldsymbol{\theta}$. Figure~\ref{fig:ddpm_diagram} illustrates the forward corruption and the conditional reverse denoising.

\paragraph{Physically grounded evaluation.}
Beyond image-fidelity scores, the generated states are assessed with physical observables computed on the nanodot --- magnetization $M$ and $|M|$, magnetic susceptibility $\chi$, specific heat $C_v$, and total energy $E$ --- and compared against the reference configurations. Together with conditioning on $\boldsymbol{\theta}$, this physical assessment and the closed-loop recovery of parameters from generated images (Section~\ref{sec:cycle_consistency}) constrain the forward generator to remain physically consistent rather than merely visually plausible.

\subsubsection{Cycle-Consistent Forward--Inverse Framework}\label{sec:cycle_consistency}

The inverse estimation of Hamiltonian parameters is intrinsically ill-posed: at finite temperature many magnetic textures are compatible with a given parameter set, so a purely image-based inversion cannot, on its own, single out a unique solution. The proposed framework closes the loop between the two models developed above to resolve this ambiguity. Both models already carry the physical parameters \emph{intrinsically} --- the forward generator is conditioned on $\boldsymbol{\theta}$, and the inverse estimator is trained to predict $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ from an image --- so we treat them as two complete, already-trained blocks, whose internal formulations were detailed in Sections~\ref{sec:deep_regression} and~\ref{sec:cvae_forward}, and compose them into a closed cycle.

\paragraph{Building blocks.}
\begin{itemize}
\item[--] \emph{Inverse model} $f_{\hat{\Theta}}:\mathbf{X}\mapsto\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ (Section~\ref{sec:deep_regression}): the deep regression network that estimates the parameters from a magnetic domain image.
\item[--] \emph{Forward model} $g_{\Phi}:\boldsymbol{\theta}\mapsto\hat{\mathbf{X}}$ (Section~\ref{sec:cvae_forward}): the conditional generator (cVAE or DDPM) that synthesizes a magnetic state from the parameters.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Closed cycle.}
Starting from a parameter vector $\boldsymbol{\theta}$, the forward model generates a magnetic state and the inverse model attempts to recover the generating parameters from that synthetic state,
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\theta}\;\xrightarrow{\;g_{\Phi}\;}\;\hat{\mathbf{X}}\;\xrightarrow{\;f_{\hat{\Theta}}\;}\;\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}},
\label{eq:cycle_flow}
\end{equation}
and the round-trip consistency is quantified by the parameter recovery error
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cycle}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \big\lVert\, f_{\hat{\Theta}}\big(g_{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\big) - \boldsymbol{\theta}\,\big\rVert_2^2 .
\label{eq:cycle_consistency}
\end{equation}
The composition is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:cycle_consistency}.

\paragraph{From degeneracy to a unique solution.}
Because the forward map is one-to-many, a given $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ admits many plausible textures while a given texture may be explained by several parameter sets. Requiring that a synthesized state map back to its own generating parameters acts as a selection principle: among the degenerate candidates, only those mutually consistent under both mappings survive. The closed loop thereby collapses the degenerate solution manifold toward a single, parameter-identifiable configuration, reinforcing the physical traceability that parameter conditioning alone cannot guarantee.

\paragraph{Closed-loop evaluation.}
The cycle is assessed on held-out parameter sets at three complementary levels, with the analysis stratified by the magnetic-phase clusters identified in the latent space (ferromagnetic, helical, labyrinthine, and skyrmionic regimes), so that identifiability can be reported per physical regime:
\begin{itemize}
\item[--] \emph{Image level:} agreement between the generated and reference states through MSE, structural similarity (SSIM), and Fourier-domain correlation.
\item[--] \emph{Physical level:} consistency of physical observables computed on the nanodot --- magnetization $M$ and $|M|$, magnetic susceptibility $\chi$, specific heat $C_v$, and total energy $E$.
\item[--] \emph{Parameter level:} the recovery error between the original $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ and the re-estimated $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$, which directly measures how well the loop resolves the degeneracy.
\end{itemize}
A small parameter-level error together with physically consistent observables indicates that the forward and inverse blocks are mutually coherent and that the framework has steered the inverse problem toward a unique, physically grounded solution.

\subsubsection{Bayesian Hyperparameter Optimization}

Each architecture introduced above --- the inverse regression network and the forward conditional generators (cVAE / DDPM) --- is governed by a set of hyperparameters $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$ that determine its capacity and training dynamics, such as the latent dimensionality, the KL weight $\beta$ and its annealing schedule, the reconstruction-term weights, the diffusion noise schedule, the network depth and width, and the learning rate.
Because the resulting loss landscape is highly non-convex and exhibits multimodal behavior arising from magnetic degeneracy, an exhaustive search is infeasible and a principled strategy is required.
Bayesian hyperparameter optimization is therefore employed to systematically search this space and identify the optimal configuration
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:hyperparameter_objective}
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\star} =
\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\lambda} \in \Lambda}
\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{J}$ denotes the validation objective measured on held-out data (e.g., a combination of image-reconstruction fidelity, parameter-recovery accuracy, and consistency of the physical observables).

\paragraph{Surrogate Modeling of the Objective.}
Instead of directly evaluating $\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})$ for every configuration, Bayesian optimization constructs a probabilistic surrogate model $M$—typically a Gaussian Process (GP)—to approximate the unknown objective function.  
Given a set of observed evaluations 
$\mathcal{D}_{t} = \{ (\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{i}, y_{i}) \}_{i=1}^{t}$,
the GP surrogate models the objective as:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gaussian_process_surrogate}
\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}) \sim 
\mathcal{GP}\big( m(\boldsymbol{\lambda}),\, 
k(\boldsymbol{\lambda}, \boldsymbol{\lambda}') \big),
\end{equation}
where $m$ is the mean function (often taken as zero) and $k$ is a covariance kernel (e.g., Matérn or squared exponential).  
The GP posterior provides both a predicted mean $\mu_{t}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})$ and uncertainty $\sigma_{t}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})$, enabling guided exploration.

\paragraph{Acquisition Function.}
Bayesian optimization selects the next hyperparameter candidate by maximizing an acquisition function $\alpha(\boldsymbol{\lambda}; \mathcal{D}_{t})$ that balances exploration and exploitation.  
A commonly used acquisition function is *Expected Improvement* (EI), defined as:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:expected_improvement}
\alpha_{\mathrm{EI}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
= \mathbb{E}\big[ \max(0,\, \mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{best}} - \mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}) ) \big],
\end{equation}
which has the closed-form expression:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:expected_improvement_closed_form}
\alpha_{\mathrm{EI}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}) 
= (\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{best}} - \mu_{t}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})) 
\Phi(Z) + \sigma_{t}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}) \phi(Z),
\qquad
Z = \frac{\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{best}} - \mu_{t}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})}{\sigma_{t}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})},
\end{equation}
where $\Phi$ and $\phi$ denote the standard normal CDF and PDF respectively.

This acquisition function assigns high values to regions where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the GP predicts low objective values (\textit{exploitation}), or
    \item the model uncertainty $\sigma_{t}$ is high (\textit{exploration}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Sequential Optimization Loop.}
At each iteration, the Bayesian optimization loop performs:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Update the GP surrogate using all observed evaluations.
    \item Select the next hyperparameter configuration:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:next_hyperparameter}
    \boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t+1}
    = \arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\lambda} \in \Lambda}
    \alpha(\boldsymbol{\lambda}; \mathcal{D}_{t}).
    \end{equation}
    \item Train the model under configuration $\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t+1}$ and compute its validation score:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:validation_score}
    y_{t+1} = \mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t+1}).
    \end{equation}
    \item Expand the dataset:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:dataset_expansion}
    \mathcal{D}_{t+1} = \mathcal{D}_{t} \cup \{ (\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{t+1}, y_{t+1}) \}.
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

The process continues until convergence or until a fixed evaluation budget is exhausted.
The final estimate of the optimal hyperparameters is:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:optimal_hyperparameters}
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\star}
= \arg\min_{(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{i},\, y_{i}) \in \mathcal{D}_{T}}
y_{i}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Grounding in our framework.}
In practice, the set of hyperparameters $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$ that defines each architecture is precisely what this procedure optimizes, and the search is run independently for every model (the inverse estimator and each forward generator). Bayesian optimization is thus the element that takes the hyperparameter configuration generating a given architecture and steers it toward its best attainable performance --- for instance, maximizing parameter-recovery accuracy for the inverse network, and reconstruction fidelity (SSIM and $L_1$) under a non-collapsed latent code for the cVAE, or sample quality for the diffusion model. The optimized blocks are then composed into the cycle-consistent framework of Section~\ref{sec:cycle_consistency}, so that the closed loop operates with each component already at its optimum.

\subsection{Specific Objective 2: Sim-to-Real Domain Adaptation}

This subsection addresses the second specific objective: incorporating domain adaptation strategies to bridge the gap between synthetic micromagnetic simulations and experimental imaging data. The methodology adapts the cycle-consistent framework to account for domain shift, ensuring robust generalization from idealized simulations to realistic experimental conditions while maintaining uncertainty quantification.

\subsubsection{Domain Adaptation for Sim-to-Real Generalization}

As established in Section~\ref{sec:sim2real}, the systematic distribution shift between simulated training data and experimental observations represents a fundamental barrier to deploying learned models in real-world magnetic domain characterization. To address this challenge, we will explore domain adaptation strategies that bridge the gap between idealized simulations and experimental measurements, while maintaining the physics-informed cycle-consistent architecture described previously.

The experimental reference datasets described in Section~\ref{sec:experimental_datasets}---including the Leeds Skyrmion Dataset (STM imaging, ~1,200 images) , Co/Pd MFM Dataset (MFM imaging, ~450 images) , and KIT 4D-STEM Magnetic Field Dataset (quantitative vector field reconstructions) ---will serve as references for adapting our simulation-trained models to realistic imaging conditions. These datasets capture characteristic experimental signatures including measurement noise, instrumental artifacts, finite spatial resolution, and physical imperfections that are absent from idealized simulations, providing essential benchmarks for validating sim-to-real transfer capabilities.

\paragraph{Domain Adaptation Approach.}

The domain adaptation strategy will combine data-level augmentation and feature-level alignment techniques to enable sim-to-real transfer. The approach will be integrated into the cycle-consistent forward-inverse framework while preserving the physical consistency enforced by the closed loop.

\paragraph{Data-Based Domain Adaptation.}
To reduce the distribution gap between simulated and experimental images, we will explore data augmentation strategies that introduce realistic variations to the synthetic training data . These may include noise injection, blur effects, contrast variations, and other transformations that mimic experimental imaging conditions. The goal is to create a more robust training distribution that better approximates the characteristics of real measurements without requiring large amounts of labeled experimental data.

\paragraph{Feature-Level Alignment.}
Beyond data augmentation, we will investigate methods for aligning the learned feature representations between simulated and experimental domains. Rather than operating at the pixel level, these techniques would match statistical properties of the latent features extracted by the encoder network. Several alignment approaches exist in the domain adaptation literature, including covariance matching, distribution distance minimization, and adversarial alignment. The specific combination of techniques will be determined based on empirical validation with the available experimental datasets.

\paragraph{Integration with Cycle-Consistent Framework.}

The domain adaptation strategy will be applied on top of the already-trained forward and inverse blocks (Sections~\ref{sec:deep_regression} and~\ref{sec:cvae_forward}), adapting in particular the inverse estimator $f_{\hat{\Theta}}$, which is the component that will operate directly on experimental images. Each block's original supervised objective is augmented with a domain-alignment term,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{adapt}} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{task}} + \lambda_{\mathrm{DA}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DA}},
\label{eq:da_total_loss}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{task}}$ is the block's supervised loss on labeled synthetic data, $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DA}}$ is the domain-adaptation loss (combining data-level and feature-level alignment), and $\lambda_{\mathrm{DA}}$ weights the alignment relative to the supervised term. The closed-loop consistency check of Section~\ref{sec:cycle_consistency} is retained, so that parameter estimates remain physically identifiable once the model is transferred to the experimental domain.

During adaptation, labeled synthetic images provide full supervision for the forward and inverse blocks, while unlabeled experimental images from the reference datasets guide the learned representations toward realistic imaging characteristics, ensuring that the model generalizes beyond idealized simulation conditions.

\paragraph{Uncertainty Quantification.}

When applying the inverse estimator $f_{\hat{\Theta}}$ to experimental images, rather than producing deterministic point estimates, we will implement uncertainty quantification methods to output parameter regions that reflect both model uncertainty and the inherent ambiguities in experimental observations.

Techniques such as Monte Carlo Dropout , ensemble predictions , or Bayesian inference approaches can provide distributions over predicted parameters rather than single values. For each Hamiltonian parameter (exchange, DMI, anisotropy, temperature), the output would consist of a mean estimate along with confidence or credible intervals that characterize the prediction uncertainty.

This probabilistic approach acknowledges that experimental images may be compatible with multiple plausible parameter configurations due to degeneracy, measurement noise, and the sim-to-real domain gap. The specific uncertainty quantification methodology will be selected based on computational feasibility and empirical calibration against validation data. \subsection{Specific Objective 3: Interpretability and Physical Validation}

This subsection addresses the third specific objective: implementing interpretability mechanisms that enable physical validation and traceability of model predictions. The methodology integrates multiple complementary techniques—post-hoc attribution, regression activation maps, latent space visualization, and physics-guided structural constraints—to ensure that learned representations align with underlying micromagnetic principles.

A central requirement of the proposed forward--inverse framework is the ability to interpret how the architectures --- both the forward generator $g_{\Phi}$ and the inverse estimator $f_{\hat{\Theta}}$ --- extract and use the physical information encoded in the magnetization images, and to verify that this behavior is preserved across the simulated and experimental domains. We combine a spatially resolved, regression-oriented attribution method (RAMs), which quantifies \emph{where} in the image each Hamiltonian parameter is inferred from, with a global latent-space analysis (UMAP) that exposes the structure of the learned representations. Standard post-hoc baselines (Integrated Gradients and occlusion analysis) are used as qualitative complements.

\subsubsection{Spatial Interpretability via RAMs}\label{sec:rams}

Interpretability methods based on activation mapping have become a foundational approach for understanding the decision-making processes of deep neural networks. By identifying the spatial regions within an input that most significantly influence a model's prediction, these methods project opaque latent representations back into a human-interpretable domain. Among these, Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) and its variants have been extensively utilized in computer vision to generate class-specific saliency maps~\cite{vinogradova2020gradcam}. 

In standard classification paradigms, given an input image $\mathbf{X}$ and a discrete target class score $y^c$, activation maps are computed by extracting the gradients of $y^c$ with respect to the $k$-th feature map $\mathbf{A}^k \in \mathbb{R}^{H' \times W'}$ of a terminal convolutional layer (where $H'$ and $W'$ denote the spatial dimensions of the feature map). The spatial importance weights are derived by globally pooling these gradients, and the final map is a ReLU-activated linear combination of the feature maps~\cite{zhang2024gradcam}. While highly effective for discrete semantic categorization, this standard formulation structurally fails in continuous regression tasks. The absence of a discrete target class removes the natural selection mechanism required to isolate the output dimension driving the interpretability process. Furthermore, directly computing gradients with respect to a prediction $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ introduces severe gradient instability; minute fluctuations in the scalar output can yield drastically different spatial attributions, failing to provide the physical consistency required for analyzing complex Hamiltonian parameters~\cite{makke2024symbolic}.

\paragraph{RAMs for CNN-based Approaches.}\label{sec:CNN_RAMs}

To overcome the fundamental limitations of discrete activation methods in highly degenerate physical systems, we introduce RAMs, a novel formulation explicitly tailored to bridge continuous parameter estimation with spatial topologies. Given an input magnetic domain visualization $\mathbf{X}$ and the optimized regression model $f_{\hat{\Theta}} : \mathbb{R}^{\breve{H} \times \breve{W}} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{\breve{P}}$, the network predicts a vector of physical constants $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} = f_{\hat{\Theta}}(\mathbf{X})$. Let $\hat{\theta}_p \in \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ denote the continuous prediction for the $p$-th target Hamiltonian parameter (e.g., the DMI strength $\tilde{D}$ or symmetric exchange $\tilde{J}$), and let $\theta_p \in \boldsymbol{\theta}$ represents its corresponding ground truth. Instead of utilizing a raw predicted scalar, we formulate a scale-invariant, error-based activation objective $\mathcal{O}_p$. To prevent the mathematical discontinuities and negative objective bounds inherent to absolute error formulations ($1 - |\theta_p - \hat{\theta}_p|$), we utilize a normalized Gaussian decay function:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:RAM_Objective}
\mathcal{O}_p = \exp\left(-\gamma_p \left(\theta_p - \hat{\theta}_p\right)^2\right),
\end{equation}
where $\gamma_p>0$ is a hyperparameter-specific precision factor determining the strictness of the error decay. This objective smoothly bounds the activation target strictly within $(0, 1]$, promoting maximum gradient response precisely when the network's prediction asymptotically approaches the true thermodynamic value, thereby isolating the topological features that positively contribute to accurate regression.

The spatial importance weights $\alpha_k^{(p)}$ for each continuous feature map $\mathbf{A}^k$ are subsequently computed by globally averaging the gradients of the error objective $\mathcal{O}_p$ with respect to the local spatial activations $\mathbf{A}_{ij}^k$:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:RAM_Weights}
\alpha_k^{(p)} = \frac{1}{H' W'} \sum_{i=1}^{H'} \sum_{j=1}^{W'} \frac{\partial \mathcal{O}_p}{\partial \mathbf{A}_{ij}^k}.
\end{equation}

These weights strictly quantify the importance of the $k$-th latent spatial feature in successfully minimizing the prediction error for the target physical parameter $\theta_p$. The resulting RAM is then formulated as:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:RAM_Formulation}
\mathbf{\mathcal{R}}_{\text{RAM}}^{(p)} = \text{ReLU} \left( \sum_k \alpha_k^{(p)} \mathbf{A}^k \right).
\end{equation}

The application of the ReLU activation ensures that only spatial features exerting a positive, stabilizing influence on the estimation of the target parameter are preserved. Finally, $\mathcal{R}_{\text{RAM}}^{(p)}$ is spatially upsampled to the original input resolution $\breve{H} \times \breve{W}$ and normalized to the interval $[0,1]$. Then, by mapping the continuous error gradients back to the input spatial magnetization field $\mathbf{X}$, RAMs allow for the direct visual and quantitative identification of the specific emergent textures that the network utilizes to deduce the underlying energetic balances. The complete workflow for this localized spatial interpretability is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:rams_pipeline}.

\paragraph{RAMs for ViT-based Approaches.}\label{sec:ViT_RAMs}
%ViT RAMs
While our standard RAMs formulation seamlessly integrates with CNNs by exploiting their innate spatial hierarchies, extending this interpretability metric to the Vision Transformers requires mapping sequence-based latent representations back to the two-dimensional physical domain. Unlike CNNs, which preserve spatial locality through sliding convolutional filters, the ViT processes the input spatial magnetization field $\mathbf{X}$ as a flattened sequence of $N_r$ non-overlapping patches, outputting a discrete sequence of latent embeddings. To evaluate ViT-based architectures within the exact same RAMs framework, we introduce a token-reshaping mechanism that bridges the one-dimensional transformer latent space with the two-dimensional topological geometry. Let the output of the terminal Transformer encoder block be denoted as $\mathbf{Z} \in \mathbb{R}^{(N_r + 1) \times D}$, where $D$ is the embedding dimension and the extra token corresponds to the global aggregation token (e.g., the \texttt{[CLS]} token) utilized by the regression head to predict the Hamiltonian parameters $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$. By isolating the $N_r$ spatial tokens, we obtain the localized sequence $\mathbf{Z}_s \in \mathbb{R}^{N_r \times D}$. Because each sequence token explicitly corresponds to a fixed physical patch of the original nanodot domain, $\mathbf{Z}_s$ can be folded back into a 2D spatial grid. Assuming square patches, the sequence is reshaped into a pseudo-feature map:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ViT_Reshape}
\mathbf{A}_{\text{ViT}} = \text{Reshape}(\mathbf{Z}_s) \in \mathbb{R}^{\sqrt{N_r} \times \sqrt{N_r} \times D}.
\end{equation}

Once projected into this tensor format, $\mathbf{A}_{\text{ViT}}$ functions structurally identically to the terminal convolutional feature map $\mathbf{A}$ utilized in the CNN baseline. The $k$-th slice of the embedding dimension ($k \in \{1, \dots, D\}$) forms the latent spatial map $\mathbf{A}_{\text{ViT}}^k \in \mathbb{R}^{\sqrt{N_r} \times \sqrt{N_r}}$. We then apply the identical scale-invariant, error-based activation objective $\mathcal{O}_p$ defined in Equation~\ref{eq:RAM_Objective}. The spatial importance weights for the ViT embeddings are computed by globally averaging the gradients of this objective with respect to the reshaped spatial tokens:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ViT_Weights}
\alpha_k^{(p)} = \frac{1}{N_r} \sum_{i=1}^{\sqrt{N_r}} \sum_{j=1}^{\sqrt{N_r}} \frac{\partial \mathcal{O}_p}{\partial (\mathbf{A}_{\text{ViT}})_{ij}^k}.
\end{equation}

RAMs for the ViT are constructed via the ReLU-activated linear combination of these reshaped embedding slices, precisely mirroring Equation~\ref{eq:RAM_Formulation}, before being upsampled to the original input resolution $\breve{H} \times \breve{W}$. This extension guarantees an architecture-agnostic evaluation of interpretability, ensuring that the isolated degenerate textures driving the model's predictions can be fairly compared across fundamentally disparate deep learning architectures.

\paragraph{Normalized RAMs for Layer-Wise and Target-Specific Analysis.}\label{sec:Nor_RAMs}

To rigorously compare the spatial activations across different Hamiltonian parameters and network depths, it is imperative to compute the RAMs utilizing strictly normalized predictions and targets. Let $\bar{\theta}_p \in [0,1]$ and $\hat{\bar{\theta}}_p \in [0,1]$ denote the Min-Max normalized ground truth and predicted values for the $p$-th physical parameter, respectively. By scaling the variables to a uniform interval, the layer-specific error objective is strictly bounded and evaluated as $\mathcal{O}_p = \exp(-\gamma_p (\bar{\theta}_p - \hat{\bar{\theta}}_p)^2)$. For a given convolutional or transformer layer $l \in \{1, \dots, L\}$, we extract the layer-specific unnormalized RAM, denoted as $\mathcal{R}_{\text{RAM}}^{(l,p)}$, following the gradient and activation protocols established in Equations~\ref{eq:RAM_Weights} and \ref{eq:RAM_Formulation}. To quantitatively evaluate and isolate the relative contribution of each distinct layer to the estimation of every physical constant, we introduce a global cross-layer and cross-parameter normalization scheme. The globally normalized activation map, $\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\text{RAM}}^{(l,p)}\in[0,1]$, is defined by scaling the layer-specific maps against the absolute maximum spatial activation observed across all $L$ layers and all $\breve{P}$ parameters:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:RAM_Global_Norm}
\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\text{RAM}}^{(l,p)} = \frac{\mathcal{R}_{\text{RAM}}^{(l,p)}}{\max\limits_{l' \in \{1,\dots,L\}, p' \in \{1,\dots,\breve{P}\}} \left( \max\limits_{i,j} \left[ \mathcal{R}_{\text{RAM}}^{(l',p')}\right]_{i,j} \right)}.
\end{equation}

This comprehensive normalization ensures that the gradient magnitudes are dimensionally consistent and comparable, preventing parameters with intrinsically higher variance or layers with larger gradient flows from disproportionately dominating the interpretability metrics. By tracking the evolution of $\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\text{RAM}}^{(l,p)}$ across the network depth, this formulation explicitly reveals how the deep learning architecture bypasses the intrinsically ill-conditioned nature of the inverse problem. In magnetic nanodots, severe structural ambiguity arises because disparate energetic combinations of the symmetric exchange ($\tilde{J}$), DMI ($\tilde{D}$), magnetocrystalline anisotropy ($\tilde{K}$), and Zeeman field ($\tilde{H}$) can yield highly degenerate states with nearly identical free energies. Then, the global normalization aim to code how the network dynamically resolves this degeneracy through a hierarchical physical decoupling. We establish that early network layers (lower $l$) exhibit high relative activations for parameters governing short-range spin interactions, capturing the localized chiral winding angles and domain wall boundaries dictated primarily by the delicate competition between $\tilde{J}$ and $\tilde{D}$. Conversely, deeper layers (higher $l$) aggregate these localized topological textures to resolve macro-scale thermodynamic stability. These terminal layers exhibit dominant spatial activations for $\tilde{H}$ and $\tilde{K}$, which govern the global topological confinement, phase transitions, and spatial alignment of emergent structures such as skyrmion cores and labyrinthine patterns. By correlating the intensity and spatial localization of $\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\text{RAM}}^{(l,p)}$ across the network hierarchy, our framework utilizes both localized boundary chirality and global domain spacing to disambiguate the underlying Hamiltonian parameters.

    \item[--] In the \emph{inverse model}, UMAP visualizes how magnetic textures with similar $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ organize in the feature space learned by the encoder, revealing the magnetic-phase clusters (ferromagnetic, helical, labyrinthine, and skyrmionic) employed in the closed-loop evaluation of Section~\ref{sec:cycle_consistency}.
\end{itemize}

This exposes global patterns such as parameter identifiability, the degeneracy of magnetization textures, and the geometry of the learned manifolds (Figure~\ref{fig:interpretability_strategy}). \section{Experimental Set Up} \label{sec:Exp}

\subsection{Computational Infrastructure}

All experiments reported in this work were conducted in a cloud-based environment using Google Colab, under a consistent computational and experiment-tracking setup applied across all architectures.

\paragraph{Computational Resources.}
Model training and evaluation were carried out on Google Colab\footnote{\url{https://colab.research.google.com}} equipped with an NVIDIA H100 GPU (97~GB VRAM), supported by 210~GB of system RAM and 230~GB of disk storage. The architectures were implemented in Python using TensorFlow/Keras, leveraging GPU acceleration for efficient training and inference.

\paragraph{Experiment Tracking.}
The training pipeline was integrated with MLflow\footnote{\url{https://mlflow.org}} for experiment tracking, versioning, and model registry, enabling systematic comparison of architecture variants and hyperparameter configurations as well as full reproducibility across runs.

\paragraph{Data Partitioning.}
The dataset $\mathcal{D}$ was partitioned into training, validation, and test subsets using a two-stage random split with a fixed random seed for reproducibility. First, $15\%$ of the total dataset was isolated as a hold-out test set; from the remaining $85\%$, a further $15\%$ of the total was reserved for validation, yielding an effective $70\%/15\%/15\%$ train/validation/test split. The training set drives gradient-based optimization, the validation set governs early stopping and model selection, and the test set is reserved for final generalization assessment. The fixed seed keeps the samples strictly independent across subsets while preserving the underlying thermodynamic distributions of the data.

\subsection{Development of the Complete Cycle}\label{sec:cycle_development}

This section reports the experimental development of the complete forward--inverse cycle. The first part, presented here, concerns the \emph{inverse problem}: the estimation of the Hamiltonian parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ from magnetic domain images, together with the interpretability metrics that make the learned mappings physically traceable. These results were obtained and consolidated in the published study associated with this thesis. The forward (direct) task that closes the cycle is presented next.

\subsubsection{Inverse Problem: Parameter Estimation Results}\label{sec:dl_results}

The predictive performance of the studied architectures (DenseNet,
ResNet, Xception, and ViT) was assessed on the held-out test set
comprising 15\% of the total dataset. Each model was tasked with
simultaneously inferring the complete extended parameter vector
$\boldsymbol{\theta}$ directly from the two-dimensional out-of-plane
magnetization image $\mathbf{X}_n$, without any auxiliary physical
information. As shown in Figures~\ref{fig:r2_metrics}
and~\ref{fig:mae_metrics}, no single architecture uniformly dominates
both metrics: the ViT attains the highest $R^2$ score for seven of the
eight Hamiltonian parameters ($T^{(0)}$, $\tilde{J}_3$, $\tilde{J}_4$,
$\tilde{K}_{an1}$, $\tilde{K}_{anS}$, $\tilde{H}_{ex}$, and
$\tilde{K}_{DM}$), while Xception achieves the highest $R^2$ for the
second-shell exchange constant $\tilde{J}_2$. Notably, despite the ViT's superior variance-explained
performance, Xception consistently produces the lowest Mean Absolute
Error across most parameters, including $T^{(0)}$, $\tilde{J}_2$,
$\tilde{J}_3$, $\tilde{J}_4$, $\tilde{H}_{ex}$, and $\tilde{K}_{DM}$,
whereas the ViT only minimizes the MAE for the anisotropy constants
$\tilde{K}_{an1}$ and $\tilde{K}_{anS}$. This complementary behavior
between $R^2$ and MAE indicates that, while the ViT captures a larger
fraction of the parameter variance and is therefore more sensitive
to the long-range correlations governing global magnetic order
Xception yields tighter absolute predictions in the dense ordered
regions of the parameter space, where local spatial structure is the
dominant predictive signal. The ViT's broad advantage in $R^2$ is
physically interpretable: by processing the magnetization field
$\mathbf{X}_n$ as a sequence of non-overlapping spatial patches and
computing multi-head self-attention scores across all patch pairs, the
ViT explicitly captures long-range correlations between distant spin
textures, such as the global chirality imposed by $\tilde{K}_{DM}$
and the large-scale ordering driven by $\tilde{J}_1$, that are
fundamentally inaccessible to purely local convolutional receptive
fields. Xception's competitive behavior, in turn, stems from its
depthwise separable convolution mechanism, which efficiently decouples
the extraction of spatial spin-texture frequencies from the
cross-channel correlations required to map those features onto the
Hamiltonian parameter space, yielding the most accurate point
predictions for the dominantly identifiable parameters.

Figures~\ref{fig:r2_metrics} and~\ref{fig:mae_metrics} reveal a
pronounced and physically interpretable heterogeneity in predictive
accuracy across the parameter space. The initial annealing temperature
$T^{(0)}$, the second-shell exchange constant $\tilde{J}_2$, and the
DMI strength $\tilde{K}_{DM}$ consistently yield the highest $R^2$
scores across all architectures, reflecting the robust and spatially
distinctive topological imprints they leave on the observable
magnetization texture: $T^{(0)}$ governs the global degree of spin
order, $\tilde{J}_2$ controls the second-neighbor exchange pathway
that modulates the domain-scale collinearity, and $\tilde{K}_{DM}$
directly dictates the characteristic wavelength and chirality of the
domain patterns. However, a seemingly contradictory observation
emerges when examining the MAE panels in Figure~\ref{fig:mae_metrics}:
despite their high $R^2$ scores, $T^{(0)}$, $\tilde{J}_2$, and
$\tilde{K}_{DM}$ also exhibit comparatively larger absolute errors in
their respective physical units --- reaching up to approximately
$1.0\,\mathrm{K}$ for $T^{(0)}$, $0.065\,\mathrm{meV}$ for
$\tilde{J}_2$, and $0.05\,\mathrm{meV}$ for $\tilde{K}_{DM}$ in the
worst-performing architecture. This apparent inconsistency is not a
failure of the models but rather a direct consequence of the
thermodynamic landscape of the dataset. As illustrated in the phase diagrams of Figure~\ref{fig:phase_diagrams}, the parameter space
contains extensive high-temperature regions where thermal
fluctuations above the effective Curie temperature $T_C$ collapse the
magnetization texture into an indistinguishable paramagnetic noise
phase, regardless of the specific values of $\tilde{J}_2$ or
$\tilde{K}_{DM}$. Within these thermally disordered regimes, visually
identical configurations correspond to widely different parameter
values, rendering the inverse mapping fundamentally ill-posed and
inflating the absolute prediction error even when the global
correlation structure --- captured by $R^2$ --- remains strong. The
MAE values for $T^{(0)}$, $\tilde{J}_2$, and $\tilde{K}_{DM}$
therefore reflect the intrinsic degeneracy of the high-temperature
paramagnetic phase rather than a systematic modeling deficiency: the
same parameter that is most accurately recovered in the ordered
low-temperature regime (where the spin texture preserves a clear
topological signature) is simultaneously the most degenerate in the
disordered high-temperature regime (where the texture collapses into
noise), producing non-negligible global MAE values driven primarily
by the ill-conditioned subset of the dataset. A rigorous quantitative
decomposition of this effect requires first separating the dataset
into its constituent magnetic structural categories, as the global
aggregation of all configurations inevitably conflates high-accuracy
ordered-phase predictions with fundamentally ill-posed
paramagnetic-regime estimates. This per-phase analysis is addressed
further ahead, where the unsupervised structural clustering of the
magnetization images allows us to isolate and quantify the specific
contribution of each magnetic phase to the observed prediction error.

Conversely, the models exhibit two qualitatively distinct regimes of
limited predictive performance for the remaining Hamiltonian
parameters, which together delimit the fundamental identifiability
boundaries of the inverse problem in the 2D observation space. The
higher-order exchange constants $\tilde{J}_3$ and $\tilde{J}_4$
constitute the most severe case: their $R^2$ scores remain near zero
across all evaluated architectures, indicating that the models are
unable to recover meaningful information about these parameters
beyond the dataset mean. Physically, the spatial signatures of these
subordinate exchange constants are energetically dominated by the
primary $\tilde{J}_1$ and $\tilde{J}_2$ interactions, progressively
masking their observable effect on the magnetization texture and
rendering them effectively unidentifiable from the $s_z$ projection
alone. Notably, however, their MAE values --- on the order of
$0.03$--$0.05\,\mathrm{meV}$ --- remain numerically comparable to
those of the better-identified parameters, which is a direct
consequence of their narrow physical range in the dataset rather than
genuine predictive skill: a model predicting the dataset mean for
$\tilde{J}_3$ and $\tilde{J}_4$ will naturally incur a small absolute
error simply because the dynamic range of these parameters is itself
limited. The magnetocrystalline anisotropy constants $\tilde{K}_{an1}$
and $\tilde{K}_{anS}$ together with the external Zeeman field
$\tilde{H}_{ex}$, in turn, occupy an intermediate identifiability
regime. This partial predictability is physically consistent: these
parameters couple primarily to the in-plane spin components $s_x$
and $s_y$ rather than to the observable out-of-plane component
$s_z$, so the models are able to recover them only indirectly through
the secondary topological imprints they induce on the chiral domain
structure, such as the modulation of stripe periodicity or the
preferential orientation of skyrmion cores. Furthermore, the visual
manifestations of surface anisotropy $\tilde{K}_{anS}$ remain
partially entangled with bulk anisotropy $\tilde{K}_{an1}$ and
geometrically confined to the boundary layer of the nanodot, while
the Zeeman contribution $\tilde{H}_{ex}$ --- with MAE values below
$0.009\,\mathrm{meV/atom}$ across all architectures --- becomes
increasingly indistinguishable from thermal disorder in the
high-temperature regime. Together, these effects render the anisotropy
and field parameters intrinsically partially observable and define the
fundamental identifiability limits of the inverse problem within the
two-dimensional $s_z$ projection.

direction in its latent representation. In contrast, the 
longer-range exchange constants $\tilde{J}_3$ and $\tilde{J}_4$ 
produce nearly uniform colorings across the entire embedding, 
indicating that the model's internal representations carry little 
discriminative information about these parameters a result that 
is fully consistent with their low $R^2$ values and their energetic

$\tilde{K}_{an1}$ and $\tilde{K}_{anS}$ and the external field 
$\tilde{H}_{ex}$ show spatially mixed and unstructured colorings, 
corroborating the partial observability limitations and the 
confounding effects identified in the regression analysis.

To systematically identify and characterize the dominant magnetic 
phases encoded in the latent space, a density-based clustering 
analysis was performed using the HDBSCAN algorithm on the UMAP 
embedding, followed by a KMeans subdivision of anomalously large 
clusters. Based on visual inspection of representative samples 
and the mean values of the Hamiltonian parameters 
$\boldsymbol{\theta}$ within each group, the resulting clusters 
were consolidated into four physically meaningful magnetic phase 
categories.

The \textit{ferromagnetic phase} groups configurations characterized by near-uniform out-of-plane magnetization distributions, where the spins are predominantly aligned along a single direction with minimal spatial modulation, reflecting the dominance of ferromagnetic exchange interactions over chiral and thermal perturbations. The \textit{others} category constitutes the most heterogeneous group in the dataset, gathering configurations that do not exhibit the defining spatial signatures of any single ordered magnetic phase. This category encompasses thermally disordered states in which thermal fluctuations have disrupted long-range spin order, transitional configurations located at the boundaries between competing phases, and topologically mixed textures whose spatial structure is ambiguous or intermediate between ordered regimes. The common characteristic of all configurations in this group is the absence of a distinctive and reproducible magnetization pattern, making them the most structurally ill-conditioned samples in the dataset. The \textit{labyrinthine \& conical phase} comprises configurations exhibiting well-defined, spatially extended stripe and labyrinthine domain patterns with clear periodicity and chiral winding, reflecting the stabilization of competing magnetic interactions into ordered mesoscopic textures at sufficiently low annealing temperatures. Finally, the \textit{helical phase} contains the most spatially coherent and regularly ordered configurations in the dataset, displaying well-defined helical stripe patterns with consistent chiral winding direction and spatial periodicity across the entire nanodot, representing the most energetically ordered magnetic ground states accessible within the sampled parameter space.

constraint that these textures are generated exclusively

target degenerate and the inverse mapping statistically
ill-posed for these parameters within these specific
structural regimes.

To spatially trace the predictive behavior of the Xception
architecture across the identified magnetic phase categories,
RAMs were computed following the
CNN-based formulation introduced in
Section~\ref{sec:CNN_RAMs}. For each of the four magnetic
phases a single representative magnetization image
$\mathbf{X}_n$ was selected as the sample closest to the
centroid of the corresponding cluster group in the UMAP
latent space, maximizing the concentration of gradient
activations within the physical extent of the nanodot disk.
RAMs were computed for the three most identifiable
Hamiltonian parameters ($T^{(0)}$, $\tilde{J}_2$, and
$\tilde{K}_{DM}$) across eight strategically selected
convolutional layers of the Xception backbone, spanning the
full depth of the network:

\begin{itemize}
    \item[--] \texttt{block1\_conv2}: entry-level features
    at spatial resolution $112\times112$, capturing
    low-level pixel-scale magnetization gradients.
    \item[--] \texttt{block3\_sepconv2} and
    \texttt{block4\_sepconv2}: intermediate entry-flow
    layers at $56\times56$ and $28\times28$, encoding
    domain boundary frequencies and local spin-winding
    patterns.
    \item[--] \texttt{block6\_sepconv2} through
    \texttt{block12\_sepconv2}: middle-flow layers at
    $14\times14$, constituting the deepest repeated
    processing stage of Xception and encoding progressively
    more abstract topological representations of the
    magnetic texture.
    \item[--] \texttt{block13\_sepconv2}: exit-flow
    transition layer prior to global pooling, encoding
    high-level semantic representations of the global
    magnetic phase.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Selection of the precision factor $\gamma$.}
The precision factor $\gamma$ in the RAM objective
$\mathcal{O}_p = \exp\!\left(-\gamma\left(\theta_p -
\hat{\theta}_p\right)^2\right)$ governs the selectivity
of the gradient signal: small values render the objective
nearly constant regardless of prediction quality, producing
spatially uninformative maps, while excessively large values
collapse the gradient to zero for parameters with
non-negligible prediction error, yielding empty activation
maps. To select $\gamma$ in a principled and data-driven
manner, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by evaluating
the objective $\mathcal{O}_p$ at the empirical mean
prediction error $\bar{\varepsilon}_p =
\langle|\theta_p - \hat{\theta}_p|\rangle$ of each
Hamiltonian parameter on the test set, for values spanning
$\gamma \in \{0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100\}$.

Figure~\ref{fig:gamma_selection} illustrates the resulting
sensitivity curves. The left panel shows the shape of the
Gaussian objective as a function of the prediction error
$\varepsilon$ for each candidate $\gamma$: values below
$\gamma = 1$ produce flat curves that activate uniformly
regardless of prediction quality, while values above
$\gamma = 20$ generate overly steep curves that suppress
the gradient for errors as small as $\varepsilon \approx
0.2$. The right panel evaluates $\mathcal{O}_p$ at the
empirical mean error of each parameter: all variables
maintain $\mathcal{O}_p > 0.5$ for $\gamma \leq 10$,
whereas for $\gamma \geq 20$ the parameters with larger
prediction errors --- specifically $T^{(0)}$ and
$\tilde{K}_{DM}$, whose mean errors reach $0.17$ and
$0.24$ respectively --- begin to approach the gradient
collapse threshold of $\mathcal{O}_p < 0.05$. The value
$\gamma = 10$ was therefore selected as the optimal
operating point: it is the most selective value that
simultaneously maintains a well-conditioned gradient
signal for all eight Hamiltonian parameters, ensuring
that the resulting RAMs are both spatially discriminative
and physically interpretable across the full
identifiability spectrum of the inverse problem.

introduced in Section~\ref{sec:ViT_RAMs}. In contrast

convolutional feature maps at varying spatial resolutions,
the Vision Transformer processes the input magnetization

pseudo-feature map $\mathbf{A}_{\text{ViT}} \in

Because the ViT architecture does not possess intermediate
spatial hierarchies analogous to convolutional layers, the
RAM analysis collapses to a single spatial attribution map
per parameter, derived from the gradient of the
error-decay objective $\mathcal{O}_p$ with respect to the
spatial token embeddings $\mathbf{Z}_s$ at the output of
the terminal encoder block.

smoother, patch-level spatial resolution consistent with

self-attention mechanism, which explicitly correlates

%\subsubsection{Comparative Analysis and Physical Interpretation}

Comparing the RAM-based interpretability results 
across Xception and the Vision Transformer reveals 
two complementary and physically consistent 
perspectives on how deep learning architectures 
resolve the inverse Hamiltonian estimation problem. 
Xception operates through a hierarchical spatial 
decomposition: early convolutional layers capture 
fine-grained domain periodicity and boundary 
chirality at high spatial resolution, while deeper 
middle-flow and exit-flow layers progressively 
consolidate these local features into coarser, 
topologically meaningful representations of the 
global magnetic phase. This hierarchical decoupling 
mirrors the multi-scale energetic competition of the 
extended Heisenberg Hamiltonian, where short-range 
exchange interactions ($\tilde{J}_1$, $\tilde{J}_2$) 
set the local domain scale while long-range DMI 
and thermodynamic effects ($\tilde{K}_{DM}$, 
$T^{(0)}$) govern global phase stability. The Vision 
Transformer, by contrast, resolves the same physical 
information through a single-scale, globally coherent 
patch-level attribution, leveraging multi-head 
self-attention to explicitly correlate distant spin 
textures across the nanodot. This architectural 
difference explains why the Vision Transformer 
achieves marginally superior overall regression 
performance --- its global receptive field more 
efficiently captures the long-range topological 
coherence that distinguishes the identifiable 
parameters $T^{(0)}$ and $\tilde{K}_{DM}$ from 
the degenerate ones. In both architectures, the 
RAM analysis isolates the same set of physically 
meaningful spatial features as the primary drivers 
of accurate regression: chiral domain wall 
boundaries, stripe periodicity, and skyrmion-like 
core structures. Conversely, both architectures 
fail to produce spatially concentrated activations 
for $\tilde{K}_{an1}$, $\tilde{K}_{anS}$, and 
$\tilde{H}_{ex}$, confirming that the limited 
identifiability of these parameters is not a 
consequence of insufficient model capacity but of 
the fundamental physical constraints of the inverse 
problem --- specifically, the partial observability 
of the anisotropy signal in the $s_z$ projection 
and the confounding of the Zeeman field with 
thermal disorder in the two-dimensional 
magnetization image.

\subsection{Proof of Concept: Conditional Variational Autoencoder with Physics-Guided Conditioning}

The proposed architecture, illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:cvae_architecture}, implements a learned conditional prior $p_\theta(z|c)$ that depends on physical parameters $c$ (temperature $T$ and the derived KDM parameter combining anisotropy and Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya interactions), rather than imposing a fixed standard normal prior as in classical VAEs. This design choice aims to structure the latent space according to the underlying Hamiltonian parameter space, thereby reducing ambiguities arising from parameter degeneracy.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{Figures/cVae.png}
    \caption{Conditional VAE architecture with physics-guided parameter conditioning. The encoder $Q_\phi$ maps input magnetic configurations $x$ to a latent distribution $q_\phi(z|x,c)$ conditioned on physical parameters $c$. The prior network $P_\psi$ learns a parameter-dependent latent prior $p_\psi(z|c)$, while the decoder $G_\theta$ reconstructs magnetic configurations from sampled latent codes and conditions.}
    \label{fig:cvae_architecture}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Loss Function Implementation}

For this proof of concept, we implemented a simplified loss function without explicit physical constraints, focusing on reconstruction fidelity and latent space regularization. The total loss $\mathcal{L}$ minimized during training is:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:poc_total_loss}
\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}(x, \hat{x}) + \beta \cdot D_{\text{KL}}\left(q_\phi(z|x,c) \,\|\, p_\psi(z|c)\right),
\end{equation}
where:

\paragraph{Reconstruction Loss.}
Mean Squared Error (MSE) measures pixel-to-pixel fidelity between the original and generated image:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reconstruction_loss}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \|x_i - \hat{x}_i\|^2.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Conditional KL Divergence.}
The KL divergence regularizes the posterior distribution by enforcing proximity to a parameter-dependent latent prior:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kl_loss}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{KL}} = D_{\text{KL}}\left(q_\phi(z|x,c) \,\|\, p_\psi(z|c)\right).
\end{equation}

Unlike classical VAEs, the conditional prior $p_\psi(z|c)$ introduces a physics-shaped structure into the latent space. Since both the encoder posterior $q_\phi(z|x,c) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2)$ and the prior $p_\psi(z|c) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_2, \sigma_2^2)$ are Gaussian, the KL divergence admits an analytical solution:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:analytical_kl_divergence}
D_{\text{KL}} = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{j=1}^{d} \left[ \log\frac{\sigma_{2,j}^2}{\sigma_{1,j}^2} + \frac{\sigma_{1,j}^2 + (\mu_{1,j} - \mu_{2,j})^2}{\sigma_{2,j}^2} - 1 \right],
\end{equation}
where $(\mu_1, \sigma_1)$ come from the encoder $q(z|x,c)$, $(\mu_2, \sigma_2)$ come from the prior network $p(z|c)$, and $d$ is the dimension of the latent space. The hyperparameter $\beta$ (Beta-VAE) controls the regularization weight, allowing a balance between reconstruction quality and latent space structure.

\subsubsection{Experimental Setup and Results}

For this proof of concept, we trained the cVAE exclusively on the KDM DATABASE SPINERS dataset, focusing on two physical parameters: temperature $T$ and the derived KDM parameter. The model was trained without explicit physics-based regularization ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{phys}} = 0$) to establish baseline generative performance and evaluate the intrinsic capability of the conditional prior to structure the latent space according to physical parameters.

\paragraph{Identified Limitations and Future Refinements.}
Despite these encouraging results, this proof-of-concept implementation reveals critical methodological challenges that must be addressed before deployment:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hyperparameter Optimization}: The current architecture was trained with manually selected hyperparameters. To fully exploit the model's capacity, a systematic Bayesian optimization procedure (e.g., using Optuna~) must be conducted to identify optimal values for $\beta$, latent dimensionality $d$, learning rates, and network depth. The baseline performance reported here likely underestimates the architecture's true potential.

    \item \textbf{Pixel-Level Metrics and Geometric Invariance}: A fundamental limitation emerged during evaluation: magnetic domain configurations are inherently invariant to spatial transformations (rotations, translations, and reflections). Two configurations that differ only by a rigid transformation are \textit{physically equivalent}, yet pixel-wise metrics such as MSE assign them high dissimilarity. This misalignment between the evaluation metric and the physical equivalence relation leads to systematically underestimated generative quality. Figure~\ref{fig:generation} qualitatively demonstrates that the model can produce physically coherent states, but quantitative assessment via MSE penalizes geometrically transformed yet physically valid outputs. Alternative evaluation strategies must be explored, including:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Rotation- and translation-invariant metrics} based on topological descriptors (e.g., skyrmion number, vorticity fields, or Betti numbers).
        \item \textit{Energy-based metrics} that assess the physical consistency of generated states through micromagnetic energy functionals rather than pixel-level comparisons.
        \item \textit{Structural similarity measures} (e.g., Fréchet Inception Distance adapted to magnetic textures) that capture perceptual and physical similarity beyond pixel alignment.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

These findings validate the feasibility of the proposed architecture and motivate the subsequent integration of physics-informed regularization to further reduce degeneracy, improve generalization to unseen parameter regimes, and ensure strict adherence to micromagnetic principles. Moreover, they highlight the necessity of developing evaluation protocols that respect the geometric symmetries inherent to magnetic domain structures.

\section{Conclusions and Future Work} \label{sec:Conclusions}

This work proposes a physics-informed deep learning framework addressing parameter degeneracy, data quality degradation, and interpretability in nanoscale magnetic domain characterization. Proof-of-concept validation on over 200,000 Monte Carlo images demonstrates that conditional VAEs with learned physics-guided priors achieve high-fidelity reconstructions while organizing latent representations according to physical parameters, and that transfer learning-based CNNs (DenseNet121) effectively estimate Hamiltonian parameters with strong temperature prediction performance. The Regression Activation Maps methodology reveals adaptive attention to topologically relevant features, confirming interpretable feature learning aligned with physical structure.

The following paragraphs detail the technical strategies proposed for each specific objective:

\paragraph{Objective 1: Addressing Parameter Degeneracy through Cycle-Consistent Physics-Informed Learning.}
To tackle the degeneracy problem—wherein multiple parameter combinations yield visually similar magnetic configurations—this work proposes a cycle-consistent framework integrating forward (generative) and inverse (parameter estimation) models. The forward pathway employs a conditional Variational Autoencoder (cVAE) trained on atomistic Hamiltonian simulations to generate magnetic domain configurations conditioned on physical parameters (exchange interaction $J$, anisotropy $K$, DMI strength $D$, external field $\mathbf{h}_{\text{ext}}$, and temperature $T$). The inverse pathway leverages transfer learning with DenseNet121 architectures pre-trained on ImageNet and fine-tuned for Hamiltonian parameter regression from magnetic textures. Cycle-consistency regularization ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{cycle}}$) enforces bidirectional coherence: generated configurations must yield consistent parameter estimates when passed through the inverse model, and estimated parameters must reconstruct the original configuration through the forward model. Physics-informed regularization is incorporated through micromagnetic energy functionals (exchange, anisotropy, Zeeman, DMI terms: $\mathcal{L}_{\text{energy}}$) and Landau-Lifshitz-Gilbert (LLG) residuals ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{LLG}}$) to ensure generated configurations are energetically stable, dynamically consistent, and respect fundamental conservation laws. This integrated approach is expected to reduce degeneracy by constraining the solution space to physically plausible parameter-configuration pairs, particularly in high-temperature regimes where DMI estimation is most ambiguous.

\paragraph{Objective 2: Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap through Hybrid Domain Adaptation.}
The simulation-to-experiment gap arises from systematic differences in imaging modalities (synthetic Monte Carlo snapshots vs. Magnetic Force Microscopy, Spin-Polarized Scanning Tunneling Microscopy, and Lorentz Transmission Electron Microscopy), instrumental noise characteristics, resolution limits, and experimental artifacts. To enable robust transfer from simulation-trained models to experimental deployment, this work proposes a hybrid domain adaptation strategy operating at multiple levels. At the \textit{data level}, physics-aware augmentation techniques will generate synthetic training samples that mimic experimental artifacts (Gaussian and Poisson noise, blur kernels, contrast variations, resolution degradation) while preserving topological invariants. At the \textit{feature level}, Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) matching and CORAL (Correlation Alignment) will minimize statistical discrepancies between source (simulation) and target (experiment) feature distributions in intermediate CNN layers. At the \textit{model level}, transfer learning will initialize networks on large-scale simulation datasets and fine-tune on limited experimental data (Leeds skyrmion dataset, Co/Pd MFM images, KIT 4D-STEM data) using few-shot learning strategies. The interplay between domain adaptation and physics-informed constraints will be investigated to ensure that alignment techniques preserve physical consistency (energy minimization, micromagnetic equilibrium) while adapting to experimental imaging conditions. Empirical evaluation will compare data-augmentation-only, feature-alignment-only, and hybrid approaches to identify optimal strategies for different domain shift magnitudes and experimental modalities.

\paragraph{Objective 3: Ensuring Interpretability through Post-Hoc Attribution and Physics-Guided Explainability.}
To validate that learned models capture physically meaningful features rather than spurious correlations, this work develops interpretability methodologies tailored to magnetic domain analysis. The core approach is Regression Activation Maps (RAMs), an extension of gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) adapted for continuous parameter regression tasks. RAMs compute spatial attribution maps highlighting image regions (domain walls, skyrmion cores, vortex centers, stripe boundaries) that most strongly influence parameter predictions. These attribution maps will be systematically analyzed across parameter regimes to verify that model attention adapts to topologically relevant structures: exchange-dominated states should highlight nearest-neighbor correlations, DMI-dominated regimes should emphasize chiral domain walls, and anisotropy-sensitive predictions should focus on out-of-plane magnetization components. Beyond RAMs, the framework will integrate energy decomposition analyses that partition total micromagnetic energy into constituent terms (exchange, anisotropy, DMI, Zeeman) and correlate spatial energy densities with model attention. Comparison with alternative explainability methods—including integrated gradients, occlusion studies, and latent space disentanglement via $\beta$-VAE factorization—will establish robustness and identify method-specific biases. Rotation and translation invariance of attribution maps will be evaluated using topological descriptors (skyrmion number, vortex counting, domain wall density) to ensure physically consistent interpretations independent of image orientation or translation.

\paragraph{Critical Limitations and Future Development Directions.}
While proof-of-concept validation demonstrates feasibility, several critical limitations guide future development. First, \textit{hyperparameter optimization} through Bayesian methods (Optuna, Hyperopt) is required to systematically tune $\beta$ parameters in the cVAE, latent dimensionality, learning rates, network depth, and prior capacity to fully exploit model capacity. Second, \textit{evaluation metrics} must be extended beyond pixel-level comparisons to include rotation/translation-invariant topological descriptors, energy-based measures from micromagnetic Hamiltonians, and perceptual similarity metrics that align with human expert assessment. Third, the \textit{integration of forward-inverse pipelines} into a unified cycle-consistent framework with full physics-informed regularization remains to be completed and validated on experimental datasets. Fourth, \textit{domain adaptation strategies} require empirical evaluation across diverse experimental modalities (MFM, SP-STM, Lorentz TEM, 4D-STEM) to quantify transferability limits and identify failure modes. Fifth, \textit{comparative evaluation} with alternative generative architectures—including diffusion models (DDPM, DDIM), normalizing flows, and transformer-based generators—will assess trade-offs in sample quality, mode coverage, training stability, and computational efficiency. Finally, extension to three-dimensional micromagnetic textures, incorporation of stochastic thermal fluctuations beyond equilibrium Monte Carlo sampling, and integration with experimental feedback loops for active learning represent longer-term research directions. The validated components establish a foundation demonstrating feasibility for addressing degeneracy and enabling interpretable, physics-consistent predictions in experimental deployment.
